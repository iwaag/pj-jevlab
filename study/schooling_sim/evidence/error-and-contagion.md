## Summary: positive and negative/qualifying results

How spread is quantified in the literature (so p2 can report the same quantities):
1. **Cascade size**: the number of individuals that respond after a single initiator, reported as a distribution (log–log) and its mean (EC-01, EC-02).
2. **Spatial extent vs time**: mean distance from the initiator of individuals that have responded by time t, and the wave speed as its slope (EC-01). In starlings: rank-ordering by turning time, x(t) = [r(t)/ρ]^(1/3), and propagation speed cs from the linear regime (EC-03).
3. **Attenuation**: the peak response intensity as a function of rank in the cascade (EC-03).
4. **First-responder model**: logistic probability of response given features of the initiator (EC-01, EC-02). Network measures of the initiator (weighted directed clustering coefficient) predict cascade size (EC-01).
5. **Contagion type**: simple vs numerical-threshold vs **fractional-threshold** (complex) contagion, fitted to observed cascades (EC-01). A Dodds–Watts dose-threshold model with one free sensitivity parameter (EC-02).

Positive (supports p2):
- In fish, a single spontaneous startle (an "error", with no external threat) propagates exactly like a real alarm. Fish cannot tell them apart (EC-01, EC-02). False alarms are therefore a natural model for "one agent errs and others copy it", and they have been measured.
- The spread is best described by **fractional** contagion: the response probability depends on the fraction of observed neighbours that responded, so neighbours that have not responded inhibit it (EC-01; PN-02 uses the same fractional form). The p2 reference rule (majority of the 3 neighbours' previous moves) is itself a fractional threshold rule, so p2 has a direct empirical analogue.
- The group's spatial structure, not the individual's rule, controls how far cascades spread (EC-02). p2 should report cascade size conditional on local density and neighbour overlap, not only on the model.

Negative / qualifying:
- Starling turning information spreads linearly in time with **negligible attenuation** (cs about 10–20 m/s). Standard first-order alignment models (Vicsek type) predict slower, diffusive, damped spread. Matching the data needed behavioural inertia (EC-03). p2's rule (copy the neighbours' previous move, no inertia) is first-order. Inference: p2 would likely give damped, diffusive spread of a single error, and that should not be generalised to real schools.
- Flocks are **more robust to quenched (frozen) disorder** than equilibrium ferromagnets: long-range order survives in 3D and quasi-long-range order in 2D (EC-04). Spatially heterogeneous media can even create an **optimal noise** level, with disorder at both low and high noise (Chepizhko et al. 2013, read in part, search log). The lab's errors are fixed to inputs, a kind of quenched disorder in input space, not physical space. No source found studies that case directly. This is the main gap.
- Not found: any collective-motion study where the agents' errors are a deterministic function of their local perceptual state, as in p2. Also not found: a direct simulation comparing correlated vs independent heading noise in Vicsek-type models. The closest work is quenched disorder (EC-04) and the N-version coincident-failure literature (S-11).

### EC-01 Rosenthal et al. 2015: startle cascades in golden shiners, fractional contagion on visual networks
1. **Source.** Rosenthal S.B., Twomey C.R., Hartnett A.T., Wu H.S., Couzin I.D. (2015). "Revealing the hidden networks of interaction in mobile animal groups allows prediction of complex behavioral contagion." PNAS 112(15):4690–4695. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1420068112 ; PMC4403201. Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: full text of the PMC main text (Introduction, Results and Discussion, Conclusions, figure legends). The SI Appendix (contagion model details, Figs. S19–S25) was not read.
2. **Local view and representation.** The visual field of each fish was reconstructed by ray-casting (occlusion). Features tested: metric distance, topological distance, angular position, angular area, ranked angular area, and others. The best two predictors of a first response were **log metric distance** and **ranked angular area** (the rank of the initiator among visible neighbours by retinal size). A visible initiator is required: fish rarely respond to occluded initiators (likelihood-ratio test P < 0.0001).
3. **Per-agent decision and dimensionality.** **Binary**: startle (fast-start) or not. P(i|j) = (1 + exp(−β1 − β2·LMD − β3·AR))⁻¹, with β1 = 0.302, β2 = −1.421, β3 = −0.126. The authors note that fish appear to perceive others' state in a binary way (responded or not).
4. **Predator model and interaction.** No predator. Spontaneous startles were used; startles caused by an alarming mechanosensory stimulus did not differ in how they propagated.
5. **Noise or error.** The "error" is a spontaneous startle (false alarm), about once every 3.3 h per individual. Spread **was measured**: cascade size distribution (broad, plotted log–log), spatial extent vs time, and wave speed (about twice the maximum swim speed, decelerating over time). The initiator's own kinematics (speed, turning rate) do not explain influence.
6. **Collective metrics, baselines, N, horizon, replicates, effect sizes.** 138 spontaneous evasion events in schools of 150 ± 4 fish. Interaction network edge weight wij = P(i|j): directed and weighted. The initiator's local weighted directed clustering coefficient predicts cascade size (GLM, LRT P < 0.0001). Weighted k-core does not (P = 0.18). High betweenness predicts less influence. Shortest path length predicts responders beyond metric distance (df = 2, N = 5502, P < 0.0001). Three contagion models (simple, numerical threshold, fractional threshold) were simulated on the measured networks: 5000 parameter sets × 50 replicates per network, with randomised-identity networks as baseline. **Fractional threshold fits best.** The most influential and most susceptible individuals are at the front and side periphery.
7. **Evidence type.** Empirical (lab tracking) plus simulation of contagion models. Data availability not checked.
8. **Relevance to p2 (inference).** Copy: (a) the single-initiator cascade-size distribution, (b) spread vs time, and (c) a comparison of simple vs fractional contagion fits on p2's interaction graph. These turn "does one error propagate" into standard quantities. p2's majority-of-3 rule is a fractional rule; with k = 3, one erring neighbour is a 1/3 fraction and should usually be outvoted. Expect small cascades unless errors co-occur in neighbours (correlated input), which is exactly what p2's fixed-to-input errors could produce. Not tested in the source: errors that are deterministic in the input, or predator-driven turning.

### EC-02 Sosna et al. 2019: risk changes cascade size through group structure, not individual rules
1. **Source.** Sosna M.M.G., Twomey C.R., Bak-Coleman J., Poel W., Daniels B.C., Romanczuk P., Couzin I.D. (2019). "Individual and collective encoding of risk in animal groups." PNAS 116(41):20556–20561. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1905585116 ; PMC6789631. Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: full text of the main text (Significance, Abstract, Results and Discussion, Materials and Methods: experiments and contagion model). SI not read.
2. **Local view and representation.** Visual fields were reconstructed. The predictors of first response are again log metric distance and ranked angular area of visible neighbours, under both baseline and alarmed conditions.
3. **Per-agent decision and dimensionality.** Binary startle, identified by thresholding speed × acceleration (work rate).
4. **Predator model and interaction.** No live predator. Perceived risk was manipulated with alarm substance (Schreckstoff) or a water control. Repeated exposure was used to cause habituation.
5. **Noise or error.** False alarms (spontaneous startles) start cascades. Spread **measured**: number of cascades, average cascade size, cascade size distribution. Contagion model: an SIR-type Dodds–Watts dose-threshold model on a network with weights wij from the first-responder logistic regression. It has one free parameter, the mean dose threshold θ̄.
6. **Collective metrics, baselines, N, horizon, replicates, effect sizes.** Groups of 40 fish in a 1.06 × 1.98 m tank, 1 h acclimation then 0.5 h after stimulus. First exposure: nearest-neighbour distance fell (t.s. = −2.04 cm, P < 0.0001, n = 7 groups), with fewer visible neighbours (t.s. = −4.47, P < 0.0001). The number of cascades rose (V = 28, P = 0.011) and mean cascade size rose (t.s. = 0.954 individuals, P < 0.0001). Third exposure: more cascades (V = 15, P = 0.029) but no change in size (t.s. = 0.060, P = 0.313) and no change in structure. First-responder data: n = 46 baseline and 108 alarmed events. Adding before/after timing did not improve the model (χ² = 3.925, P = 0.270). The fitted thresholds θ̄ before and after overlap (95% credible intervals). Larger cascades need the change in spatial positioning; a change in responsiveness alone does not produce them.
7. **Evidence type.** Empirical plus model fitting. A Dryad dataset exists (doi:10.5061/dryad.sn02v6x5x, seen in search results only; not opened).
8. **Relevance to p2 (inference).** Copy the factorial decomposition: hold the decision model fixed and vary the structure (density and neighbour overlap), then hold the structure fixed and vary the model. This separates "the model's errors spread" from "the geometry makes any error spread". It qualifies a naive p2 reading: a difference in error spread between decision models can be confounded by different school structure produced by those models. Compare cascade sizes on matched configurations.

### EC-03 Attanasi et al. 2014: turning information in starling flocks spreads linearly with little attenuation
1. **Source.** Attanasi A., Cavagna A., Del Castello L., Giardina I., Grigera T.S., Jelić A., Melillo S., Parisi L., Pohl O., Shen E., Viale M. (2014). "Information transfer and behavioural inertia in starling flocks." Nature Physics 10:691–696. arXiv:1303.7097 (arXiv title: "Superfluid transport of information in turning flocks of starlings"). Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: full text of the arXiv version (main text, Fig. 1–2 captions, Table I). The theory appendices were skimmed only. Journal details are from search results and Wikidata (not checked against the journal page).
2. **Local view and representation.** Empirical, 3D. The theory is an alignment model with inertia (spin and curvature variable). The interaction network is not the focus.
3. **Per-agent decision and dimensionality.** Continuous heading. A turn is identified by the maximum in radial acceleration.
4. **Predator model and interaction.** None directly measured. Predator threat (peregrine falcon) is the motivation.
5. **Noise or error.** Not modelled as error. Spread of a localised change of direction **was measured**. Birds were ranked by pairwise turning delays τij. Rank r vs time t gives a propagation curve x(t) = [r(t)/ρ]^(1/3). The first 5 birds to turn are close together, so the turn starts locally. Peak acceleration intensity vs rank gives the attenuation.
6. **Collective metrics, baselines, N, horizon, replicates, effect sizes.** 12 flocks (N from 50 to 595), filmed at 170 Hz. x(t) has a **linear** regime, so the speed cs is in the range 10–20 m/s (Table I: 9.41–21.32). Attenuation is negligible. cs grows with polarisation Φ: the rescaled speed cs/a is linear in 1/√(1−Φ) (a is a model constant whose definition was not checked; P = 3.1 × 10⁻⁴, R² = 0.74, one point per flock). The authors state that current (first-order, Vicsek-type) theories predict "slower and dissipative" transport. Table I polarisations are 0.757–0.959; the authors note these are lower than earlier 10 Hz estimates because of noise.
7. **Evidence type.** Empirical plus theory (an inertial spin model). Data not linked in the read text.
8. **Relevance to p2 (inference).** Defines how to measure "how far one change spreads": rank by onset time, spread radius vs time, attenuation vs rank. p2 can apply the same measures to the spread of an injected error or of a predator-triggered turn. Contradiction: p2's alignment is first-order (copy the neighbours' previous move), the model class this paper says gives damped, diffusive spread. So p2's temporal-spread results describe that model class, not real schools. Higher polarisation should speed up spread; report Φ alongside.

### EC-04 Toner, Guttenberg & Tu 2018: flocks with quenched (frozen) disorder stay ordered
1. **Source.** Toner J., Guttenberg N., Tu Y. (2018). "Swarming in the dirt: ordered flocks with quenched disorder." PRL 121, 248002; arXiv:1805.10324. Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: partial (abstract, Introduction, hydrodynamic set-up, conclusions). The scaling derivations and the companion long paper were not read. Related: Chepizhko O., Altmann E.G., Peruani F. (2013). "Optimal noise maximizes collective motion in heterogeneous media." PRL 110, 238101; arXiv:1305.5707. Read level: abstract, introduction, model definition and Fig. 1–2 captions.
2. **Local view and representation.** Toner et al.: continuum (hydrodynamic) theory plus Vicsek-type simulations. Chepizhko et al.: metric Vicsek-type alignment plus avoidance of obstacles within radius Ro.
3. **Per-agent decision and dimensionality.** Continuous heading.
4. **Predator model and interaction.** None. Chepizhko et al. use static or diffusive obstacles, which particles turn away from.
5. **Noise or error.** Toner et al.: a quenched random force f(r) that depends only on position (frozen in space, the same for every particle at that place), in addition to ordinary annealed noise. Chepizhko et al.: annealed angular noise η plus quenched heterogeneity (obstacles). Error spread between agents is not measured.
6. **Collective metrics, baselines, N, horizon, replicates, effect sizes.** Toner et al.: long-range order persists in d = 3 and quasi-long-range order (power-law velocity correlations) in d = 2. Equilibrium ferromagnets get only short-range order in d = 2. The 2D simulations agree with the theory and with the earlier simulations of Chepizhko et al. and Das et al. Chepizhko et al.: order parameter r vs η and obstacle density ρo (L = 140, ρb = 1). An **optimal noise amplitude** exists. At high obstacle density, order is quasi-long-range and exists only between two critical noise values. Example states: r = 0.58 (η = 0.01, clustered), 0.97 (η = 0.3), 0.73 (η = 0.6, bands) at ρo = 2.55·10⁻³.
7. **Evidence type.** Theory plus simulation.
8. **Relevance to p2 (inference).** This is the closest physics analogue to "errors fixed to particular inputs". p2's errors are quenched in *input (perceptual) space*, not physical space. As agents move, they pass through different views, so the error each agent makes changes over time and is shared by all agents in the same view. The result that flocks tolerate quenched disorder, and that adding annealed noise can help (optimal noise), suggests two hypotheses p2 can test with its two baselines: (a) the school may tolerate fixed-input errors better than an equal rate of random errors, or worse, if the errors line up with predator geometry; (b) adding independent noise on top of fixed errors may improve order. Neither is tested in these sources.
