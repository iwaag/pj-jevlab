# Second passes, re-asking, voting, coherence across question forms, and confidence cascades

Theme summary. Eight recent arXiv papers (submitted 2026-09 to 2026-10) bear on what the lab learned in set4/set5/set6 about asking a decision model again, voting, and gating. The strongest convergence is that a one-pass scorer's second pass adds nothing (2610.07716), that a cheap decision model and an LLM make the same confident mistakes (2609.29769), and that confidence cascades save cost but add little or no accuracy and fail exactly where the first stage is confidently wrong (2609.26550, 2610.07177, 2609.24574). Three further papers (2609.33971, 2609.33209, 2609.37470) show that logically equivalent question forms give different probabilities and even different actions, which is a label-free check the lab has not used yet.

All eight were read from the pdftotext output (full text for the body; long appendices skimmed or not read, so appendix-only numbers are marked "abstract only" or "not verified in body"). Accessed 2026-10-08. "Hosted Jev" = TypeSafe jev-1.13 via API. Kev in these papers is mostly the 0.8B/4B/9B family, not Kev-27B (K27).

## 2610.07716 — Readout Stability in Prefill-Only Decision Models: Zero-Label Prediction and Inference-Time Compute Allocation
- Ran Li, Lei Chen (HKUST / HKUST-GZ); arXiv v1 submitted 2026-10-06; https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07716; read: full text (PDF via pdftotext, no appendices in the text file), accessed 2026-10-08.
- Systems and readouts: open System-One models only, no hosted Jev. Seven families: kev (0.8B, 4B, 9B; pointer head), Open-Jev (2B, 9B; pointer head, vLLM), decider (0.8B, 2B), this-that, Mica-4B, Tiny-Jev, plus the Qwen3-4B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B generative controls read through next-token option scores. Readout = per-option scores over a menu in one forward pass.
- Design: 91 menu-only interventions (random, avoidance-style, retrieval, alphabetical, self-selected menus; sizes 2 to 30), 16 datasets incl. CLINC150, TREC, MMLU, ARC, CSQA, OBQA, SciQ, AG News, DBpedia, GoEmotions, SST-5 (ordered scoring); English; typically 500 test items (CLINC150 500; cascade tests on 250 held-out and 2,000 held-out for label-table mapping). Pre-registration: none stated. Stats: mean abs error of predicted vs measured accuracy; one-sided sign tests (p = 0.016 avoidance vs random; 7/7 sources p = 0.0078).
- Key results (section/figure):
  - Restriction predictor (restrict pass-1 probabilities to the menu, renormalise, take argmax; no labels, no second pass) predicts post-intervention accuracy to within 4.2 points over the whole family-by-source grid (Sec 3.4, Fig 3); 24 of 35 context-free cells within +-2 pp. Open-Jev-2B is exact (residual 0.0 on rand15, avoid15, self clean) because its second pass is a restriction of its first (Table 1). Other families have signed residuals, e.g. kev-0.8B +0.5/+1.7/+6.8/-0.8/+9.8 for rand15/avoid15/self clean/self verify/self 2 (Table 1).
  - Label-free constants err by 18.0 and 18.7 points, about 15x worse (Sec 4.1).
  - The property is ordinal: predicting accuracy from the pass-1 probability errs by 21.0 points, from the pass-1 ranking by 1.33 (Sec 4.2, Fig 3). So calibration does not recover it.
  - Class-specific: generative Qwen3-4B/8B err by 1.6 to 15.8 points (decision models about 0.5) and the larger model errs more (Sec 4.3).
  - Residual drivers: closest-rival probability (rho = 0.75) and menu size (self-selected menus residual +9.8 at 2 candidates, +2.0 at 30); label count itself rho = 0.14 (Sec 3.4, Fig 4). Residual inverted-U up to +14.2 at C=3 (Sec 4.4).
  - Uniform extra passes (order-permuted, K up to 32) change accuracy by at most 3.83 points on any source (pooled 1.2); only MMLU moves significantly; expected calibration error falls 0.09 to 0.03 by K=2. 32 passes of 0.8B reach 46.0% on MMLU vs 72.4% for one 4B pass (Sec 5.1, Fig 5).
  - Confidence cascade (least-confident fraction to a larger sibling) beats re-asking at matched cost: 0.684 at 1.28 units vs 0.668 for best re-ask at 1.44; 0.760 at 2.40 vs 0.652 for order marginalisation at 2.00 (CLINC, kev-0.8B to 4B, 250 items). Across 7 sources a three-tier kev cascade (0.8B to 4B to 9B) reaches 9B accuracy at 1.0 to 6.3 forward units instead of 11 (Table 2). The kev gate beats random routing on 4 of 4 extra sources by 2.0 to 7.2 pp; the decider family's gate does not beat random on every source (Sec 5.2, Fig 5).
  - Hierarchy re-ask fails: TREC 50-class two-stage 0.270 vs flat 0.368 (Fig 6). Menu curation: 0.8B on a curated 5-candidate avoidance menu 95.4% vs 4B on flat 150 labels 80.0% (but avoidance menus require the gold label to be known offline); random 15-candidate menu 86.4% vs 69.0% flat (Sec 5.3).
  - Wording moves Open-Jev-2B accuracy over 26 points (0.454 to 0.714) on a fixed menu; shuffling the same menu moves it at most 0.6; direction reverses across families (comparative phrasing costs Open-Jev 9.4, kev -0.8) (Sec 5.4).
  - Mapping instead of re-running: merging near-duplicate labels by mapping cached answers gains 5.2 points (2,000 held-out CLINC items); re-deciding in the merged space loses 4.2 (Sec 6).
- Proposed method / recipe and cost: probe battery (~10 GPU-minutes per new family) to test re-ask information and wording direction; offline ranking of menu strategies from cached pass-1 distributions (seconds); curate menu first, then escalate by a label-free quantile threshold, then abstain; use 2 permutation passes only for calibration.
- Limitations (authors): inference-time only; predictor exact for one family and tight for others; on emotion and product label spaces the residual is a fixed +2.5 to +4.9; per-item flip model is a postdiction with a small calibration set; presentation effects not explained; retrieval-front-end recall assumed measurable. My caveats: 0.8B to 9B models, short-text intent/topic/MCQ tasks, not agentic policy workloads; no missing-evidence or fallback class; no pre-registration; "avoidance" menus need gold labels; gate quality depends on family (decider gate not reliably better than random).
- Relevance to the lab: set5 (K27 second pass); set4 (verification/vote); lab item 8 (permutation averaging). Verdict: **already shows what the lab found (prior/parallel work)** for set5, and **extends the lab's finding** with a cascade/menu-curation recipe. Reusable idea: for the K27 pointer head, (1) treat any menu-only re-ask as free to simulate from cached first-pass scores, so never spend a second pass on top-2/top-3 narrowing; (2) test the "size-up cascade" with K27 as first stage and a different, larger or differently trained model as second (W12/A27 letter logits or hosted Jev), gated by K27 margin; (3) run the paper's ten-minute probe (wording direction, order, restriction exactness) as a standard first test of any new system.

### Comparison (a): 2610.07716 versus the lab's set5
- Same result? Essentially yes. Paper: for menu-only interventions the second pass is predicted by restricting and renormalising the first pass, so re-asking a prefill-only model on a sub-menu has nothing to add (exact for Open-Jev-2B; residuals of a few points for kev, decider, Mica, Tiny-Jev). Lab set5: K27 second-pass probabilities equal the first-pass probabilities renormalised over the offered options (median difference about 0.01), and top-2, top-3, top-1-vs-fallback, and first-choice-named re-asks changed at most 3/120 answers.
- Models/families: paper = Kev 0.8B/4B/9B (the same family as K27, pointer head), Open-Jev, decider and others, all small, all prefill-only scorers; the hosted Jev and Kev-27B are not tested. Lab = Kev-27B only on policy/priority/sufficiency workloads (W3), not intent classification. So the lab result extends the family to 27B and to harder agent workloads; the paper adds a quantitative error table and a counter-example class (generative models).
- Differences: the paper's claim is accuracy-level (argmax after restriction), the lab's is both answer-level and probability-level (median diff 0.01); the paper explicitly says the property is ordinal and the probability-level estimator fails by 21 points (menu-change probabilities are systematically too high after a change, relative to measured accuracy). That sits oddly with the lab's finding that renormalised probabilities match second-pass probabilities; the two are not in conflict because the paper's probability-level estimator predicts accuracy from mean pass-1 probability of the gold label, not second-pass probabilities directly.
- What they recommend instead: confidence cascade to a larger sibling model (not re-asking), menu curation (avoidance/retrieval menus), mapping label tables rather than re-running, and two permutation passes only for calibration. The lab's set5 conclusion was only negative ("nothing to work with"); its set4 tested wording votes and verification but not escalation to a different scorer.
- Dates (neutral): lab set5 report dated 2026-10-03; paper v1 submitted 2026-10-06. Parallel discovery; neither cites the other and there is no reason to think the paper saw the lab's data.

## 2609.29769 — Jev vs. LLMs as Rubric Judges: Cheaper, Faster, and Wrong in the Same Places
- Delip Rao, Chris Callison-Burch (UPenn); v2 dated 2026-09-28; https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29769; read: full text (body; appendices A to P skimmed only through body cross-references), accessed 2026-10-08.
- Systems and readouts: hosted Jev (TypeSafe jev-1.13; Choice, Noul and Score primitives; cost $0.042 per million input tokens) versus three flash-tier LLM judges (GPT-5.6 Luna, Gemini 3.8 Flash, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash), per-criterion and whole-rubric modes, medium and high reasoning effort. No open decision models.
- Design: 9 panels from 7 benchmarks with human labels (RiceChem, HealthBench binary; ELLIPSE, FED-Turn, FED-Dialogue, HelpSteer2, LFQA, USR-TC, USR-PC ordinal), 5,003 pairs, English, identical criterion texts, no examples or tuning; thresholds for cascades "oracle" (chosen on test) or cross-fitted over 50 halvings. Exploratory interpretation after expectations were formed on binary panels; not pre-registered.
- Key results:
  - Jev's accuracy differs significantly from the LLM judges in at most 8 of 27 paired comparisons per setup; ahead mostly on binary checklist criteria, behind only on ordinal ones (abstract; Table 3). Cost of LLM judges 16 to 325x Jev; wall time 28 to 350x (Table 3).
  - On all 7 ordinal panels judge-to-judge QWK exceeds judge-to-label QWK (Table 2b); offsets are negative for all matched judges on 6 panels (Table 2c). ELLIPSE: all four matched judges wrong on 64.0% of pairs; shifting one level lifts Jev Choice from 13.4% to 50.4% exact (Sec 5.1).
  - Correlated errors: on Jev Choice's 12 most confident errors per ordinal panel (84 pairs), 242 of 252 LLM verdicts (96.0%) repeat its wrong answer, versus 50.3% under independence (Sec 5.3, Table 3; 96.8% and 97.2% in the whole-rubric and high-effort conditions). Over all Jev errors the share beats the independence baseline on every ordinal panel, by 18.8 to 34.2 points on six and 8.6 on ELLIPSE. Repeats and the excess peak in Jev's top confidence band (80.5% to 92.8% vs 33.6% to 63.7%, Fig 3b).
  - Jev confidence ranks its own errors on 6 ordinal panels (AUROC 0.57 to 0.70; 0.49 on ELLIPSE; 0.41 on LFQA factuality). On binary panels AUROC 0.80 to 0.86, ECE 0.056 to 0.096 (Sec 6.1).
  - Cascades: cross-fitted gain over the best single judge at most 1.5 points (HealthBench), cascades trail the best single judge on 6 of 9 panels by 0.1 to 7.3 points; oracle gain at most 2.0 (2.7 across all conditions) and not significant in a kept-pair sign test (Sec 6.2, Table 4). Cost: 16% to 48% of the best LLM's cost for 0.2 to 1.8 points less accuracy on six panels. Equation (1): cascade gain over fallback arises only on kept pairs Jev alone gets right and the fallback gets wrong, and those are few (Jev alone right on 12 to 37 pairs of an ordinal panel).
  - Jury: the LLM judges' median never beats the best single judge, trailing by up to 12.8 points (ELLIPSE); Jev as a fourth juror never helps significantly (Sec 6.2). More reasoning effort did not make the LLM errors less like Jev's.
- Proposed method / recipe: compare the share of repeated errors with the independence baseline on the labelled pairs that set the threshold, before building a cascade (Appendix P, not read); design future decision models for complementary errors.
- Limitations (authors): observational account; no exemplars or rewritten rubrics; LLM runs at different dates (8 days apart); small spot checks for repeat noise (about 3% of Jev pairs flipped across runs); no larger fallback tried. My caveats: "errors correlate" is partly explained by shared offsets and unstated rating conventions, i.e. rubric-text underspecification, not by shared model weakness; ordinal rubric scoring on human-labelled panels, not policy decisions with a gold branch; flash-tier judges only.
- Relevance to the lab: set6 p1, set4, lab item 2 (missing evidence), item 6. Verdict: **extends the lab's finding** (correlated errors between different model classes, and a quantitative recipe for the "no useful second voter" result). Reusable idea: apply the independence-baseline test to the lab's own models. For each K27 confident error (p >= 0.8), compute the share of W12/A27/L27 verdicts that repeat it versus the rate at which each gives that same wrong answer on other cases of the same class; this tells whether a cross-readout cascade or panel can help before running it. The readout it applies to is any pair (K27 pointer head, W12/A27 letter logits, L27 generated).

### Comparison (b): "wrong in the same places" versus the lab's set6 p1 and set4
- Paper: 96.0% of LLM verdicts repeat Jev Choice's confident errors (independence baseline 50.3%); that is cross-class (a probability-only classifier versus three reasoning LLMs from three vendors).
- Lab set6 p1: 24 K27 agents differing only by persona are worth n* = 1.0 effective votes (mutual-error agreement 0.90 to 1.00), whereas a panel of 7 different systems has n* 3.4 to 5.6 of 7. Lab set4: a 3-model majority (W12, K27, A27) made things worse.
- These are partly consistent and partly in tension. Consistent: same-weights populations are fully correlated (paper's own point that errors correlate across providers too, citing Kim et al. 2025 and Goel et al. 2025), and set4's majority did not help. In tension: the lab's mixed-model panel is much less correlated than 96% (n* 3.4 to 5.6 of 7), whereas the paper finds near-full agreement between its classes on confident errors. Plausible reasons (untested): the paper counts only Jev's 12 most confident errors per panel (extreme tail, high agreement, binary-ish ordinal scales with shared offset conventions), while the lab's n* is estimated over all cases; the paper's tasks are rubric scoring against human labels with ambiguous conventions, while the lab's are agent workloads with a gold policy answer; and the paper's baseline "50.3%" is high because the comparison is conditioned on label and criterion. The lab's own "missing evidence" error (all systems decide anyway) matches the paper's tail picture. Recommended reconciliation test: compute the lab's n* restricted to each system's confident (p >= 0.8) errors.

## 2609.37470 — Probability Contracts: Accuracy, Coherence, and Decisions Across LLM Interfaces
- Han Chen, Yingrui Li (independent); v2 dated 2026-10-05; https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37470; read: full text, body Sections 1 to 7 (appendices not read), accessed 2026-10-08.
- Systems and readouts: hosted Jev-1.13.0 (Noul = "Event" binary and Choice interfaces; probabilities as serialized decimals); open Kev-4B (typed pointer head over supplied options); Qwen3.5-4B Base and Posttrained (answer-ID token likelihood, renormalised, FP32). Appendix adds Mistral/OLMo (not read).
- Design: 1,000 synthetic "worlds" (250 per generator, 125 per generator-encoding stratum; four generators), exact posterior known by rational arithmetic; 12 requests per root (canonical, complement, conditional, intersection, product, permutation, rendering, opaque keys, metadata, Event, binary Choice, exact repeat); 11,913 eligible requests; one attempt each; English. Prompts, thresholds, main cohort fixed before collection; Jev criterion, calibration, disagreement analysis, and Sections 7 and Appendices P to S post hoc. Reject-option controller with defer cost 0.10 (also 0.02, 0.05, 0.20, 0.40; 99-cost grid); 5,000 root bootstrap.
- Key results:
  - Accuracy and coherence rank systems differently. Canonical posterior TV: Kev lowest, then Jev, Posttrained, Base; paired Jev minus Kev difference 0.0335 [0.0222, 0.0448] on 990 roots (Sec 6.1). Kev wins four of eight strata (joint-attribute, noisy-sensor), Jev four (missing-rule).
  - Table 2 (mean relation residual, lower better; Kev / Base / Posttrained / Jev): Event vs Choice 0.0629 / 0.2138 / 0.1126 / 0.1938; complement 0.5055 / 0.1155 / 0.8000 / 0.0929; coarsening 0.3554 / 0.2451 / 0.2836 / 0.1751; permutation TV 0.1143 / 0.3224 / 0.3470 / 0.0534; exact repeat 0 / 0 / 0 / 0.0188 (Jev repeat pairs passing tolerance 745/1,000).
  - Event versus Choice for the same binary event change the action (act vs defer, at defer cost 0.10) on 14.9% of roots for Kev, 34.2% Posttrained, 32.8% Jev (all 328 Jev changes are act/defer crossings); Base always defers (Table 3). Introductory example: true 0.694, Jev Event 0.84, Choice 0.97, gives defer vs answer-yes, expected loss about 0.306.
  - Jev fails the pre-set operational criterion (validity >= 0.995 and relation pass rate lower bounds >= 0.95); the criterion applied symmetrically to local configurations also fails (post hoc).
  - Disagreement between two reports of the same event certifies only 11% to 52% of mean pair error (the half-gap lower bound; Sec 7.1). Disagreement-based selection at matched 50% coverage beats random retention for Jev and Posttrained but beats confidence selection only for Jev, and all 50%-coverage policies exceed always-defer loss at c=0.10: a configuration-specific diagnostic, not a general improvement (Sec 7.1).
  - Averaging the two interface reports (Sec 7.2): reduces expected Brier by (p-p')^2/4, and reduces loss versus a random interface at c=0.10 by 0.03463 (Jev), 0.00959 (Kev), 0.05297 (Posttrained); but versus Event alone, averaging raises Jev loss by 0.00236 [0.00077, 0.00411]. Brier improvement is the average of loss over uniformly distributed costs, so a better Brier score can coexist with higher loss at a given cost (proof in Appendix R, not read).
  - Kev defers on 96.1% of canonical roots yet incurs loss 0.1090, above always-defer (0.1000); Jev loss 0.1336, oracle regret 0.0438 (Table 3).
  - Jev assigns exactly zero mass to possible outcomes in 20 of 990 valid canonical reports (infinite log loss).
- Proposed method / recipe: "probability contract" benchmark (declared event maps and relations) plus two-interface averaging or disagreement as selection signal; results say neither is a reliable decision improvement. Cost: two queries per item.
- Limitations (authors): synthetic four-cell worlds; one served Jev version, one collection run; no reasoning arms produce valid reports; Appendix A secondary cohorts; findings configuration-dependent. My caveats: Kev here is the 4B head, not K27; defer-cost framing is binary event decisions, not the lab's multi-option agent decisions; "Posttrained" Qwen probabilities collapse on complement (0.80).
- Relevance to the lab: lab items 3 (order sensitivity), 4 (verification/vote), and set5. Verdict: **new idea worth trying** (label-free coherence check) and **background only** for numbers. Reusable idea: for K27 pointer head, build a "complement and permutation" probe: score the same decision as a K-option choice and as K binary yes/no questions via L27/W12 letter logits, measure the action-change rate at the lab's fixed defer threshold. This costs two passes but gives a no-label signal, and the paper's warning is that it should be measured per configuration, not assumed useful.

## 2609.33971 — Do System One Decisions Add Up? A Study of Probabilistic Coherence
- Saman Sarker Joy (Universiti Malaya); v1 submitted 2026-09-27; https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33971; read: full text through Sec 7.4 (Appendices A to D not read), accessed 2026-10-08.
- Systems and readouts: hosted Jev (jev-1.13.0, Choice interface) and the English Laya root checkpoint (ModernBERT-large, about 421M, local FP32; encoder score with released option-count temperature). No Kev.
- Design: 2,500 matched examples per system from TREC (500, 6 parents, 50 fine), CLINC150 (1,000 in-scope, 10 parents x 15), MASSIVE en-US (1,000, 18 parents, 60 fine), K+2 questions per input (coarse, flat, one conditional per parent), 72,000 classification questions, English, shuffled option order; 10,000-replicate paired bootstrap, no multiplicity adjustment; order audit on 90 examples (30 per dataset, 4 permutations); repeat audit of Jev on the same 90. Pre-registration: not stated.
- Key results:
  - Reconstruction through the hierarchy (marginalisation u(parent) x v(child | parent)) vs flat: Jev accuracy falls 91.0 to 68.1 on CLINC150 (-22.9 [-24.9, -20.9]), 72.2 to 60.4 on TREC, 83.3 to 66.9 on MASSIVE; Laya rises 23.1 to 44.4 on CLINC150 (+21.3 [18.0, 24.5]), 28.8 to 34.4 on TREC, 23.8 to 33.0 on MASSIVE (Tables 3, 4).
  - Mean parent total variation between asked and summed probabilities: Jev 0.219 (TREC) to 0.349 (CLINC150); Laya 0.424 to 0.689 (Table 6). Flat and marginal select the same label for Jev in 69% to 76% of examples, Laya 12% to 36%.
  - Hard routing: Jev on CLINC150 has 317 wrong-parent errors and only 37 wrong-child errors; conditional accuracy given the right parent 94.6%.
  - Option-order flips (flat labels): Jev 20.0% (TREC), 5.8%, 5.8%; Laya 62.5%, 61.7%, 76.7%; marginal flips Jev 17.5%, 19.2%, 14.2% (Table 7; 120 non-independent comparisons from 30 examples). Jev repeated identical requests: 1 of 60 TREC flat flips, none on the others.
  - Laya MASSIVE: accuracy +9.2 but ECE rises 0.046 to 0.124.
- Proposed method / recipe: none; recommendation to validate the exact decomposed workflow and to keep conditional and unconditional confidence separate. Cost: K+2 queries per decision.
- Limitations (authors): decomposition changes instructions, candidate set, number of choices and order at once; no wording ablation; label descriptions for TREC are abbreviation-based; Laya's option-count temperature clamping; cherry-picked extreme cases; no importance weighting. My caveats: parent-level question phrasing (e.g. "utility" example) can cause errors unrelated to coherence; tests a different signal (hierarchy) from the lab's; the lab's workloads are not hierarchical.
- Relevance to the lab: lab items 3 and 5 (breaking a decision into steps). Verdict: **new idea worth trying** (cheap label-free probe) and **contradicts nothing**; it supports the lab's set5 conclusion by showing that re-asking in a different form is not a restriction (different question text) and changes answers in either direction. Reusable idea: for K27, test whether a two-stage route/branch decision (policy class first, then option within class) gives the same distribution as the flat decision; compute parent TV on the lab's W1 routing workload and use it as an uncertainty flag; compare "marginalise over all branches" with "route by top branch" (the paper finds marginalisation better in all 6 model-dataset cells).

## 2609.33209 — Beyond Calibration: Do a Typed-Decision Model's Probabilities Obey the Probability Axioms?
- Keyi Li, Yihao He, Quanyi Li (Northeastern University, Shenyang); v1 submitted 2026-09-27 (doc dated 2026-09-29); https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.33209; read: full text through Limitations/Conclusion (appendices not read), accessed 2026-10-08.
- Systems and readouts: hosted Jev (one version, via a gateway; Noul yes/no and Choice) versus Qwen3.8-27B (official BF16 weights, thinking disabled; the lab's L27 base model) under two readouts: first-token yes/no probability and a verbalized JSON probability.
- Design: 160 items (ChaosNLI and PubMedQA, 3 mutually exclusive labels each; English), 10 questions per item (is X_k, is not X_k, is X_i or X_j, which label), 480 negation pairs, repeats and bundled-request controls, no labels needed; 95% bootstrap CIs over items. Pre-registration: not stated.
- Key results:
  - Negation complementarity |s_k + n_k - 1|: Jev 0.064 [0.055, 0.072]; Qwen first-token 0.293; Qwen verbalized 0.122. Gap persists after averaging Jev repeats, excluding double-negation labels, and on pairs where both systems give similar probabilities (abstract, Sec 5.1 to 5.2).
  - Jev not coherent: about 5x its repeat-noise floor; mean probability of a single label statement 0.380 versus the coherent 1/3; the three single-label probabilities sum to 1.14 on average and exceed one on 159 of 160 items; Jev yes/no vs choice differ by 0.091 (Table 3). Qwen first-token single-label probabilities sum to 0.71.
  - Table 3: disjunctive complement 0.054 (Jev) vs 0.343; partition additivity 0.141 vs 0.319; cross-format 0.091 vs 0.131; monotonicity violation share >0.05: 8.4% vs 14.5%. Jev not better on disjunction additivity (0.138 vs 0.158) or normalised yes/no-vs-choice agreement (0.096 vs 0.085).
  - Failure signatures differ (Table 4, Sec 5.4): Qwen first-token under-endorses complements whether phrased "not" or as a disjunction (-0.169, -0.232 vs -0.095 single), says no to both a statement and its negation in 196 of 480 pairs, and is no more coherent where confident; Jev's violations concentrate where it is uncertain (Fig 3, ~0.4 violation for 0.5-confidence region).
  - Bundling all ten questions in one request changes Jev's answers no more than a repeat (0.0116 vs 0.0118).
- Proposed method / recipe: the label-free battery itself; practical rules: do not mix probabilities from different question forms in one decision rule; do not treat per-option yes/no probabilities as a distribution; use the choice question for partitions. Cost: ten short queries per item.
- Limitations (authors): 160 items, two domains, one wording, no paraphrase control (complement wordings differ by 0.060, about as much as the negation violation, so some of this is wording sensitivity); single LLM, thinking disabled; one Jev version; coherence is necessary, not sufficient (constant 1/3, 2/3 answers pass). My caveats: first-token yes/no readout from a general LLM is not the lab's L27 short JSON generation; three-label partitions only.
- Relevance to the lab: items 3, 4 (blind yes/no verification caught 4/17 in set4), and L27 control. Verdict: **new idea worth trying**, and **background** for set4. Reusable idea: set4's blind yes/no verification could be diagnosed with these identities: for K27 and W12/A27, compare the yes/no probability for the chosen option, the "not X" probability and the choice-form probability, and use the coherence gap, rather than the verdict, as a gate. The paper's finding that Jev (and the lab's decision models likely) over-endorses single-option statements (sums above 1) predicts why blind yes/no verification accepts wrong answers. Readout: W12/A27 letter logits (to ask yes/no) and K27 pointer head (single-option scoring) are untested; encoders J/G would give an "over-endorsement" sum test directly.

## 2609.26550 — JEV-as-a-Judge: Accept When Confident, Escalate When Unsure
- Yubo Li, Yidi Miao, Ramayya Krishnan, Rema Padman (Carnegie Mellon); v4 dated 2026-10-06 (first version September 2026); https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26550; read: full text, body sections 1 to 10 (appendix tables partially skimmed), accessed 2026-10-08.
- Systems and readouts: hosted Jev 1.13 (label probabilities, confidence q = max probability, averaged over both candidate orders for pairs) against 16 other judges: 14 generative LLMs (incl. GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.4, Gemini, GPT-OSS 120B, Qwen3.5/3.6/3.8 27B), two reward models (Skywork-V2-8B, PairRM), and Laya checkpoints. All generative judges answered under Jev's output contract (verdict plus label probabilities, no rationale). In the cascade comparison also GPT-4.1 mini, Qwen3 32B and Qwen3.5 27B as alternative first stages.
- Design: 5,172 base judgments: RewardBench 1,500 pairs, JudgeBench 350, HaluEval 3,000, final-answer adjudication 150, RM-Bench 500 prompts x styles x orders, RewardBench 2 100, summaries 400, prose 200; blinded human adjudication of 183 of 990 disputed items; English; frozen two-order cascade policy with threshold chosen on 96 pilot pairs; pre-specified prospective live test on 570 held-out pairs from two new workloads (PPE correctness 400 held-out of 500, JudgeBench Claude split 170). Pre-registration: partial (frozen policies, human adjudication primary outcome, prospective test); slice analyses, sweeps, alternative first stages retrospective.
- Key results:
  - Pooled over 4,850 public items Jev is eighth of fifteen LLM judges, 1.7 points behind GPT-6, 277x cheaper ($0.044 vs $12 per 1,000), 0.15 s median latency. Within 3 points where the verdict can be read off the text (RewardBench 92.5 vs 92.5; HaluEval 87.3 vs 88.4); behind by 14.6 on JudgeBench (78.6 vs 93.1), 13.5 on RM-Bench hard (76.6 vs 90.1); near chance on reference-free prose (53.5) (Table 2).
  - Where Jev's two-order confidence is at least 0.9, Jev and GPT-6 are nearly tied (94.7% vs 94.4% on 3,744 items). Confident errors: 12% to 21% of Jev's preference errors vs 25% to 41% of GPT-6's (Sec 7.1). AUROC of confidence for error detection: 0.88 RewardBench, 0.73 JudgeBench, 0.83 HaluEval, 0.82 pooled (Fig 5); 0.870 / 0.839 / 0.764 on RM-Bench easy / matched / hard pairs (Sec 7.4).
  - Frozen JEV to GPT-6 cascade (tau = 0.9) on 1,610 held-out pairs: accepts 68.5%, escalates 31.5% (which hold 85% of Jev's errors); accuracy 93.4% vs GPT-6 alone 92.5% (+0.93 [0.24, 1.66]) at 41.4% of its fee (Table 4). By benchmark: RewardBench +1.27 [0.45, 2.03] at 26.7% of fee; JudgeBench -0.74 [-2.22, 0.74] at 66.8% of fee (escalates 65%).
  - The gain occurs because errors are complementary there: two-order Jev gets 60 RewardBench pairs right that GPT-6 misses and GPT-6 54 that Jev misses; on JudgeBench GPT-6 corrects 60 of Jev's 75 errors, Jev corrects 9 of GPT-6's 24, but 15 of Jev's 75 errors are also GPT-6's (Sec 8). An oracle picking the better judge would reach 95.7% on JudgeBench.
  - Threshold rule does not always transfer: GPT-5.6 Sol fallback with selected tau=0.7 loses 4.81 on JudgeBench [-8.52, -1.48]; fallbacks no more accurate than Jev never escalate; GPT-OSS 120B and Gemini 3 Flash as first stages lose 7.41 and 4.44 on JudgeBench; Skywork margin gate gains 2.84 on RewardBench but loses 6.67 on JudgeBench (Table 3).
  - Prospective test (Table 5, 570 pairs): deployed per-workload threshold (lower-bound rule, 100 selection pairs each) matches GPT-6 exactly (90.2% vs 90.2%, 0.00 [-0.70, 0.70]) at 75.5% of fee; counterfactual frozen tau=0.9 loses 0.53 [-1.58, 0.35] at 62% of fee. So on workloads where Jev alone trails by 14 points the cascade only reaches parity and the saving shrinks to a quarter.
  - Averaging two candidate orders adds little: +0.6 points (single 90.2 to 90.9) on held-out pairs; +2.0 on JudgeBench (neither significant); but 3 of 95 order-flips occur at q >= 0.9 (Sec 7.3 / Fig 7a), so flips sit below the gate. Temperature scaling from small selection sets is workload-dependent (fitted T 0.65 on RewardBench, 2.15 and 4.45 on JudgeBench and HaluEval; NLL improves on HaluEval 0.496 to 0.304 but worsens on JudgeBench 0.449 to 0.475) (Sec 7.2).
  - Where it fails: style-adversarial pairs (confident-error rate at q >= 0.9 rises from 1.5% to 7.9%); IFEval pairs AUROC 0.57; reference-free prose, where all judges are near chance but confident.
- Proposed method / recipe and cost: "accept when confident, escalate when unsure": average the verdict over both presentation orders, choose the threshold on about 100 local labels with a one-sided lower confidence bound on the accuracy loss (mean difference minus 1.645 sd/sqrt(n) >= -0.02), validate on held-out items from the same workload. Fee 26.7% to 75.5% of the fallback alone, depending on workload.
- Limitations (authors): single hosted Jev version; judges differ in size, effort, and serving (not compute-matched); offline simulations of fees; only serial escalation; thresholds set for an accuracy tolerance not a cost ratio; human adjudication by team members on a disagreement-selected subset (183 items); held-out pool 83% RewardBench. My caveats: gains rely on a reasoning fallback model that is not a decision model; 5.7% to 12% of cases are label-noise-affected (HaluEval); no missing-evidence class.
- Relevance to the lab: set4 (unanimity or review no better than random), item 5 (second pass), and item 8 (margin-based gates). Verdict: **extends the lab's finding** (a margin gate to a different, stronger system works where first-stage errors are low-confidence; fails where errors are confident). It does not contradict set4: set4's gates were all within the same family or weak voters. Reusable idea: run a K27 to W12/A27/L27 cascade on the lab's W3 data: threshold on K27 first-pass top-1 probability or top-1 minus top-2 margin, escalate to a differently trained system; report where escalation helps (conflicting records, low confidence) and where it cannot (missing-evidence, confident). Use their lower-bound threshold rule with the lab's 100-case pilot, and both-order averaging for the K27 pointer head.

RM-Bench and JudgeBench intervals contain chance. Qwen3-8B 0.611 to 0.778, Skywork reward model 0.764 to 0.976; all preregistered gaps significant.

## 2609.24574 — Evaluating Decision Models for Text Annotation in Computational Social Science
- Hazem Ibrahim, Yasir Zaki (NYU Abu Dhabi); v2 dated 2026-09-23; https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24574; read: full text through Section 6 plus appendix routing/table headers (long appendices B to E skimmed), accessed 2026-10-08.

  - Calibration (Table 2): Jev median ECE 0.157, better than verbalised confidence of 16 of 19 LLMs but worse than Opus 5 (0.066), Fable 5.1 (0.073), Sonnet 5 (0.075). ## Cross-paper notes

(c) Cascade results and when they fail
- Where cascades helped: Jev to GPT-6 on RewardBench (+1.27 [0.45, 2.03] at 26.7% of fee; 2609.26550 Table 4), Jev to Gemma 4 31B (0.638 vs 0.626; 2609.24574 Sec 4.5), kev-0.8B to 4B and three-tier kev cascade (2610.07716 Table 2). Common condition: first-stage errors sit at low confidence, and the partner is at least as accurate and has different errors.
- Where they failed or only saved cost: ELLIPSE, FED, HelpSteer2 etc. in 2609.29769 (best gain +2.0 points oracle, +1.5 cross-fitted, trailing the best judge on 6 of 9 panels), because the LLM judges repeat 96% of Jev's confident errors and by Equation (1) gain exists only on kept pairs Jev gets right and the fallback gets wrong; JudgeBench and style-adversarial pairs (2609.26550: only parity at high escalation rates, thresholds failing to transfer); near-chance first stage (2610.07177: 92.3% to 100% escalated, equal to random escalation); empathy and tasks with confident wrong answers (2609.24574).
- Pattern: a cascade needs (i) a first stage whose confidence ranks its own errors, (ii) a fallback that errs elsewhere. 2609.29769 gives the quantitative test for (ii); 2609.26550 and 2609.24574 show (i) can hold on one task and fail on the next, so thresholds must be set per workload on ~100 local labels with a lower-bound rule.

(d) Coherence violations as a cheap label-free check
- 2609.33209: negation, disjunction, partition additivity and yes/no-vs-choice gaps need no labels; Jev has them at 0.064 to 0.141; the violation is mostly a systematic over-endorsement of single-label statements (0.380 vs 1/3), so it is also a calibration-in-the-large signal.
- 2609.33971: hierarchy reconstruction changes accuracy by -22.9 (Jev, CLINC150) or +21.3 points (Laya) and the sign cannot be predicted from coherence alone; coherence and accuracy are different properties.
- 2609.37470: disagreement between equivalent requests certifies only 11% to 52% of the pair error, and as a selection signal beats confidence only for Jev (not for Kev or Posttrained); averaging helps Brier but not necessarily decision loss at each cost. So coherence checks are a diagnostic of which question forms can be combined, not a reliable error gate. Together with 2609.33209's "violations concentrate where uncertain" (Jev) versus "not more coherent where confident" (Qwen first-token), the check seems to carry the same information as confidence for Jev and different information for general LLM readouts: unproven for K27.
- All three use Jev (hosted); only 2609.37470 includes a Kev (4B), whose Event/Choice gap is smallest (0.0629) but whose complement residual is largest-but-one (0.5055). There is no evidence on Kev-27B, Winnow, AutoJev.

Evidence map to lab items 4, 5, 6
- Item 5 (set5 second pass): 2610.07716 (direct parallel), 2609.33971 (re-asking in a different form is not a restriction).
- Item 4 (set4 wording vote, verification, 3-model majority): 2609.29769 (juries of diverse judges no better than the best single judge; median trails by up to 12.8 points), 2609.33209 (yes/no over-endorsement), 2609.37470 (disagreement selection, averaging).
- Item 6 (populations of one model): 2609.29769 ("Kim et al. 2025" cited for cross-provider error correlation; tail agreement 96%).

## What the lab should not repeat
1. Do not spend more passes on a K27 narrowed re-ask (top-2, top-3, fallback-vs-top-1, first-choice-named). 2610.07716 independently predicts and measures that null; simulate any menu-only variant offline from the cached first pass instead.
2. Do not build a second-voter scheme from models likely to share errors (same weights, same training data) without first running the independence-baseline test on confident errors (2609.29769 Sec 5.3); set6 p1 and set4 already say the same.
3. Do not read a good calibration number (ECE after temperature scaling, 2610.07177) or a good Brier score (2609.37470) as evidence that a gate will work; test coverage and error at the gate threshold per workload class.
4. Do not combine probabilities from different question forms (yes/no per option versus choice; flat versus hierarchical) in one decision rule without a coherence check (2609.33209, 2609.33971).
5. Do not run cascades or gates on a class where the first stage is confidently wrong at near-base-rate accuracy (missing-evidence in the lab, empathy in 2609.24574): neither threshold choice nor temperature scaling fixes it.
6. Do not copy thresholds across workloads or fallbacks (2609.26550 Table 3: tau selected on RewardBench-heavy pilot failed on JudgeBench).
7. Treat the "avoidance menu" result in 2610.07716 (0.8B at 95.4%) as an oracle upper bound: it needs the gold label to sample distractors, so it is not a deployable menu strategy; the deployable counterpart is retrieval-style menus with a measured recall threshold.
