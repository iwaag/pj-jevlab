# Evaluation methodology for typed decision models: an evidence audit with a checklist, and a self-audit of a paired evaluation

Theme summary. Two papers are about how to evaluate typed decision models rather than about a new model. 2609.32160 audits the first 28 papers (19-24 Sep 2026) and derives a 14-item checklist from their recurring failures; its main finding is that the typed readout itself has not yet shown an independent accuracy advantage, because most studies include no label-log-probability baseline. 2610.02267 is a paired evaluation of Laya and Jev on agent-harness decisions whose author re-derived his own first-version numbers and found four errors that changed deployment recommendations (all in the accounting around the model, not in the scored predictions). Both bear directly on how the lab should report set4/set5-style gating results.

Reading status: both papers read in full from pdftotext (2609.32160 including Appendix B coding criteria and the per-paper coding table; reference lists and Appendix A skipped). Lab status judgements below are made only from the lab summary written for this survey from the episode reports under `devdocs/episodes/` and are marked where the context does not say.

## 2609.32160 — Typed Decision Models: An Early Evidence Audit and Evaluation Checklist
- Authors: Lijuan Tang, Yuemeng Zheng (Northeastern University). Submitted 26 Sep 2026 (v1). https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32160 ; read: full text (PDF via pdftotext), accessed 2026-10-08.
- Systems and readouts tested: none run. It reviews 28 papers: 22 evaluate hosted Jev (all named versions are 1.13, snapshots 20260917), 5 build open or alternative typed decision models (TDMs), 1 analyses public GitHub projects. Table 2 lists documented open readouts: decoder letter or label-token logits restricted to the options (JevLite, Visual Jev, SemIf-4B, PixelJev, Qwen3-0.6B-RLCD replication), answer-slot hidden state scored against output-embedding rows (this-that-model-1.0), Laya mask-marker embedding, Open-Jev (DeBERTa) mean-pooled over option name and definition.
- Design: arXiv search on 24 and 25 Sep 2026 (`all:jev` plus abstract searches), 29 hits, 1 excluded (a renamed-URL mention) and 1 unrelated economics paper dropped, leaving 28; each paper read in full and coded against a fixed schema (comparators, headline accuracy, latency and cost with source locations, calibration, label-probability baselines, escalation, statistics, validity threats). Extraction assisted by LLM tools; a second pass without access to the first re-checked every quantitative claim and produced two factual corrections. Checklist items coded in two steps (applicability, then compliance); "unclear" excluded from both counts; five items left unscored (T1, T4, R1, R3, C2). No inferential statistics of its own.
- Key results (Sec. 5, Tables 4-6, 8):
  - Finding 1: 21 of the 27 papers that evaluate a model have no label-probability baseline (one more unclear); matched comparisons that exist point to no independent readout advantage: typed ensemble AUROC .974 vs generative fine-tune read through label tokens .950 (n.s.; matched single-seed typed model .972, same ECE .050 after temperature scaling) [Ren et al.]; dedicated typed head vs ordinary LM-head both .761 macro accuracy [Yu and Yao]; restricted option-logit readout equals greedy generation whenever the generated answer is valid [Zhou et al.]; open interface not more robust to answer-preserving context than a plain label scorer (flip rates 72.6% vs 64.9%, different item sets) [Xu]; an untrained 4B model read by letter logits has the highest median macro-F1 among open systems [Ibrahim and Zaki].
  - Finding 2: parity often occurs on saturated tests (100/100 on 20 choices; 63/63; 212-216 of 216), while hosted Jev trails stronger models on discriminating tests (median 11.6 macro-F1 points on social-science annotation; 5.8 accuracy points on ContractNLI; 14.6 on JudgeBench; 19.8 on hard RM-Bench pairs; up to 16.4 on graded rubric criteria). Optimised answer-preserving context redirects 61.4% of Jev's initially correct decisions (16.9% for one target-aware addition, 2.2% neutral); score-guided injection raises attack success 1.8% -> 3.5%.
  - Finding 3: single hosted-Jev requests mostly take 0.15-0.45 s, fees tens to hundreds of times below frontier models; caching can shrink a median paired latency gap below 1 ms; seven papers report no latency; two sibling papers report opposite cost directions for the same comparator.
  - Finding 4: calibration inconsistent per model and workload: probabilities overstate prevalence (4.8% vs 2.7%, calibration slope 1.63; two-decimal output grid limits labels rarer than 1%); held-out recalibration reduces error 3.3x; pooled ECE 0.047 vs median per-benchmark ECE 0.168 in one study; wrong tool calls with mean confidence .778.
  - Finding 5: confidence-based escalation is the recurring deployment pattern; savings 27-72.7% in some studies, but thresholds chosen on a selection set did not hold on held-out items for every fallback model, and when the stronger model shares the TDM's errors escalation recovers little (LLM judges repeat Jev's wrong answer in 96% of verdicts on its 12 most confident errors per panel; replayed cascade gains at most 1.5 points, 2.0 with oracle thresholds). The one study with a cheap generative cascade baseline found the TDM cascade cheaper only on its own benchmark and the author retired the superiority claim on tau2-bench.
  - Finding 6: 12 of 27 report neither intervals nor significance tests for headline results; at least seven make post-evaluation design, recipe, metric or threshold choices; three add comparators after seeing results; at least seven use synthetic data in part of their evaluation; at least three have internal numerical inconsistencies; one has 100% format compliance beside 55.4% accuracy.
  - Compliance counts (Table 6, among papers to which the item applies): B1 5/26 (1 unclear), B2 1/5, B3 7/13, T2 17/26, T3 1/27, R2 15/27, C1 13/17, C3 7/7 (3 unclear), C4 3/20.
- Proposed method or recipe and its cost: the 14-item checklist below plus a minimal reporting card: exact model version and run date; test difficulty or model spread; one ordinary-LM readout baseline; one cheap generative baseline; intervals from repeated runs; held-out calibration; and, when a cascade is used, a frozen threshold evaluated on a new workload or fallback model. Cost: most items are cheap (a label-log-probability baseline, repeated hosted runs, held-out calibration, frozen cascade thresholds).
- Authors' stated limitations (Sec. 8): small, very young corpus (28 papers in about nine days, nearly all the same hosted version); a snapshot with several new papers a day; arXiv-only search; keyword search may miss systems that do not mention Jev; several checklist items need judgement and five were left unscored; conclusions about mechanism come from open TDMs only. My caveats: extraction was LLM-assisted (though double-checked); the per-item counts depend on the coders' applicability rules (e.g. B2 counts only threshold-based hand-offs, T3 requires option-name swapping across rubrics); the paper audits others' reporting, not their correctness.
- Relevance to the lab: all lab experiments (as an external yardstick for reporting), especially set4/set5 (gating, verification, majority and unanimity rules) and set3 (comparison across 7 systems). Verdict: **extends the lab's finding** on readouts (Finding 1 is consistent with the lab's observation that selection is strong (88-98%) for the LLM-based systems and that the shared failure is missing evidence rather than readout; the lab context does not contain a same-backbone readout comparison) and **new idea worth trying** as a reporting template. Concrete reusable ideas: (a) B1: add a same-backbone untuned letter-logit baseline for K27 (Qwen3.8-27B, the K27 backbone) to separate the readout and fine-tuning effects from the backbone; (b) T3: option-name swap across rubrics as a test beyond the lab's opaque-key test; (c) C3: freeze any margin gate's threshold before evaluating on the next workload class; (d) the minimal reporting card for every lab report. Readouts: B1 and T3 apply to W12/A27 letter logits and K27 pointer head; C1-C3 apply to all.

## 2610.02267 — Fast Models, Slow Evidence: A Paired and Self-Audited Evaluation of System-1 Decision Models for LLM Agent Harnesses
- Author: Jiawei Li. Submitted 1 Oct 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02267 ; read: full text (PDF via pdftotext, to the end of the limitations), accessed 2026-10-08. Appendix tables and figures (A-D, Fig. 7) not read.
- Systems and readouts tested: Laya 0.3.21 (open weights, ModernBERT mask-marker head, routes to English or multilingual checkpoint) and Jev (hosted, jev-1.13.0) zero-shot; a reference LLM (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, temperature 0, different prompt, not part of the paired comparison). Confidence = max_k p_k for choice and max(p, 1-p) for yes/no, not the systems' own confidence field (Laya's differs from max_k p_k by 0.299 on average vs 0.010 for Jev).
- Design: 11 agent decision points built from 18 public sources: 7,283 base cases plus 6,640 robustness variants (abstract). A 2,100-case suite (A-F: model routing from RouterBench, tool selection from BFCL, tool-call guard, RAG relevance from BEIR x5 + BM25, injection guard, large label sets from BANKING77/MASSIVE) disjoint from a 100-case pilot; an agent-infrastructure suite G-J (1,280 cases: injection in two channels, groundedness from HaluEval-QA, pre-execution safety, PII egress); control tracks B2, G2, J2; a policy-labelled two-tier safety track S (603 items). Byte-identical inputs to both systems; Wilson intervals; exact McNemar tests with asymptotic CI for paired differences; no multiplicity correction (claims have p < 0.001, "tie" only when p > 0.4 with narrow CI); cross-hardware (Apple M4 CPU vs RTX 2080 Ti) and cross-day reproducibility.
- Key results: determinism: Laya 0 changes on 420 reruns, Jev 1; cross-hardware Laya reproduced 1,180/1,180 (max |dp| = 0.006); a day later Jev changed 5 of 1,080 decisions (0.5%). Accuracy: Jev 76.7% vs Laya 63.1% on the 2,100-case suite (455 vs 169 discordant wins), 80.2% vs 66.0% on agent infrastructure; Jev significantly more accurate on 9 of 11 decision points (+10.8 to +46.0 pp; largest on large label sets, 81.0% vs 35.0%, +46.0 [38.7, 53.3]); model routing is a tie at chance (Jev answers "cheap" for 99.5% of requests; Laya AUROC 0.48) and RAG relevance a well-powered tie (61.0% vs 60.5%, +0.5 pp, CI [-2.0, 3.0], n = 1,920). Option order: reversing the option list changes 30.3% of Laya's decisions (53% with 60-77 labels) vs 1.8% for Jev; Laya picks "cheap" for 58.5% of routing requests in the original order and 82.3% reversed. Wording: instruction rewording and case resampling move accuracy 5-15 pp per track for both systems (a ±5 pp band); the ranking is robust (Jev leads in 8/8 wording-by-sample combinations on B, C, E, F). Capacity: Laya 100% at K = 2 and 53.8% at K = 50 with random tool distractors (each option truncated to max(4, (192-16)/K) tokens); with nearest-neighbour distractors Laya 54.0% overall and 31.3% at K = 50; Jev 84.5% overall, 98.9% on the 70.2% of items with a unique correct tool (Laya 61.6%). Calibration: pooled ECE 0.158 (Laya) vs 0.143 (Jev) on A-F and 0.198 vs 0.080 on G-J; 26% of Laya's answers with confidence >= 0.9 are wrong; Platt scaling fitted on half the data reduces Laya's ECE on the other half from 0.22 to 0.027 (Jev 0.21 to 0.10) on RAG relevance. Gates: at a 5% miss budget Laya keeps 92.4% of LLM calls and Jev 79.8% on the RAG gate; the ceiling on savings is set by base rates (a perfect gate saves at most 58% in this mix); both systems recognise under 40% of "no answer here" real top-5 contexts, so the gate is unsafe as a skip-gate (recommended as a trigger for more retrieval). Cascade Laya (conf >= 0.9) -> Jev keeps 41.7% of decisions local and saves 45% of Jev spend but lowers accuracy from 76.7% to 70.7%. Latency: Laya 31 ms p50 on RTX 2080 Ti and 200 ms on M4 CPU; Jev 325 ms from one client site and 83 ms from another; Jev 759 tokens per decision, about $32 per million decisions at $0.042/M tokens.
- Proposed method or recipe and its cost: a re-derivation protocol: re-derive every number from raw outputs and design a control experiment for every suspected confound (G2 channel vs content, B2 similar distractors, J2 natural redaction); report gates by call rate and held-out miss rate; include every component's cost; test channel effects with content native to each channel; choose thresholds out of sample. Recommendations for harness builders: use a strong System-1 model for tool selection, large-label intent classification, injection screening of tool outputs and groundedness checks; do not use zero-shot System-1 models for LLM routing, RAG-skip gating, PII screening or as the only safety gate; for Laya keep K <= about 10 dissimilar options, fix the option order or vote over permutations, compute confidence as max_k p_k, and recalibrate per domain. Cost is human time; all raw responses are released.
- Authors' stated limitations: one version of each system; English-centric decisions (multilingual cells n = 10); zero-shot only; Laya's fine-tuning path and its typed-decisions checkpoint untested (the latter crashed on his GPU); routing labels come from a single run per model (79% of A items from multiple-choice benchmarks, 95% with binary per-item outcomes); label noise (blind LLM judge agrees with the dataset on 90.0% of items both models got right but only 12.7% of items both got wrong; strict-vs-lenient judging flips 28.1% of re-judged both-wrong labels); safety/PII gold uses LLM annotators (72.8% agreement with dataset labels); synthetic mix; latency measured from two client sites and two hardware types; an audit only covers the questions its auditor thought to ask. My caveats: the benchmark is built from public datasets Jev may have seen (the author says so); absolute accuracies carry a ±5 pp wording band.
- Relevance to the lab: set4 (verification, majority, unanimity, review gates), set3 (missing evidence), benchmark p3 (order reversal on J and G), set6 p2 cost accounting. Verdict: **extends the lab's finding** (Laya 30.3% reversal flips adds a third system class to the lab's J/G flips of 35% and 23%; both Laya-like encoders recognise <40% of no-answer contexts, consistent with missing-evidence failure) and **new idea worth trying** (the self-audit pitfalls below as a pre-publication checklist). Concrete reusable ideas: (a) for J and G, test whether reversal flips are position-driven (Laya's "cheap" rate moved from 58.5% to 82.3% with order, i.e. follows position not content); (b) run the K-scaling test (accuracy as a function of K with random vs nearest-neighbour distractors) to see whether J/G truncate options; (c) cross-fit threshold selection and report the 95th percentile of held-out miss rate across splits rather than the in-sample rate; (d) report call rate and held-out miss rate for any gate built from K27/W12/A27 margins. Readouts: (a)-(b) apply to the J/G encoders; (c)-(d) apply to K27 pointer head and W12/A27 letter logits.

| Pitfall | What went wrong in the first version | After audit | What the lab should check |

| Suspected confounds that did not matter | tool-selection 99.5% could be inflated by random distractors; PII AUROC 0.41 could be caused by placeholder negatives | with nearest-neighbour distractors Jev is 98.9% on unambiguous items and the gap to Laya widens (+37.4 pp); replacing placeholders with natural phrases leaves Laya's AUROC at 0.40 | report controls that failed to change the result; they strengthen the conclusion |

Paper's closing statement: the errors that changed recommendations sat in the accounting around the model, not in the scored predictions.

### 2610.11392 — TypedBench: A Benchmark for Calibration, Framing Sensitivity, and Cost in System One Decision Models
- Read: abstract only (fetched from `arxiv.org/abs/2610.11392`, accessed 2026-10-13), not the PDF; claims below are the abstract's own, not checked against the paper body.
- Claim: TypedBench is built from seven policy-labelled generators and nine evaluation suites, explicitly to give typed decision models (named: Jev) a benchmark with "a clear reference" for accuracy and calibration, reporting accuracy as median/range across paraphrases and calibration error against the finite-sample noise floor of a matched, perfectly-calibrated predictor; it also scores probability quality (selective prediction, ordinal proper scoring rules) and realised cost under asymmetric cost matrices, across a hosted model, an open encoder, and open decoders (0.8-9B). Headline results: the hosted model (Jev) follows its stated policy but is wording-sensitive and systematically underconfident, and under asymmetric costs using its probabilities can give a worse decision than just taking its top answer; decoders route exactly but slow down and lose accuracy as options/questions grow, and are least accurate specifically on policy questions.
- Relevance to the lab: directly touches the **"per-type calibration, especially score"** open question (`study/README.md`) and the benchmark-design checklist already tracked here (2609.32160's 14 items, 2610.02267's self-audit above): TypedBench's "clear reference" framing and policy-labelled generators are a new design point for exactly the gap 2609.32160 flagged (most typed-model evaluations lack a labelled-reference baseline). The "probabilities worse than top-answer under asymmetric cost" result is new and specific: it says calibration quality and decision quality under a cost matrix can diverge, which the lab's set4/set5 cascade and gate work has not separately measured (those measure accuracy and call count, not realised cost under an explicit cost matrix). Verdict: **extends** the evaluation-methodology theme with a benchmark purpose-built for the calibration/framing/cost combination; does not test K27, W12, A27 or J/G, so transfer is unknown as with the rest of the catalog. Worth a full-text read if the lab designs a cost-weighted gate, specifically for the asymmetric-cost-matrix methodology and whether TypedBench's generators and reference labels are reusable.
