## 0. Summary of positive and qualifying results

These are source claims unless marked Inference.

**Positive (mechanism works under stated conditions)**
- Majority voting extracts a signal from noisy independent judgements (Condorcet; restated in C-08 and C-09). In an agent-based model of honeybee nest-site choice, 200 scouts reached the best of 5 sites in 98.4% of 250 runs under the strong consensus criterion with high individual reliability and high interdependence; both independence and interdependence were needed (C-08).
- A small informed minority can steer a group without anyone knowing who is informed, and a larger group needs a smaller informed proportion (model, C-05). Uninformed members dilute an opinionated minority and return control to the majority (theory plus fish experiment, C-06; abstract only).
- Local filtering rules can keep normal agents in agreement against a bounded number of arbitrary (adversarial) agents without identifying them, if the graph is sufficiently redundant (theorem, C-02). With purely oral messages, agreement among loyal generals is possible if and only if more than two thirds are loyal (theorem, C-01).
- Noisy averaging converges to the right mean in expectation but trades bias against variance; a decaying-weight design removes the trade-off at the cost of convergence speed (theorem, C-03).
- Noise-driven order-disorder transition: in the Vicsek model, global alignment appears as the noise amplitude falls below a critical value (simulation, C-04).
- Segregation from mild preferences: agents who accept up to roughly half or more unlike neighbours still produce strongly segregated patterns (Schelling, C-10).

**Negative or qualifying**
- The Vicsek order transition is discontinuous (first-order-like), not continuous as first reported; the apparent critical scaling was a finite-size effect (C-04). Hence the system near the noise threshold is bistable and its behaviour jumps, which matters for any "turn the noise knob" steering idea (Inference).
- Adversarial and random faults need different tools. The 3m+1 and (2F+1)-robust conditions are worst-case bounds for arbitrary behaviour (C-01, C-02), while the noisy-consensus results assume independent zero-mean noise (C-03). Neither bound transfers directly to a repeated model whose errors are shared (Inference).
- Larger groups are not always more accurate. When information is spatially or temporally correlated, a finite and often small group size maximises accuracy; only a minority of the modelled environments showed monotone wisdom of crowds (C-09, model).
- Interdependence amplifies errors without independence: in the bee model, with low independent exploration, consensus still emerges but not reliably on the best site (C-08).
- A strongly opinionated minority can dictate the group choice in a wide range of conditions unless uninformed individuals are present (C-06, abstract). Steering by a few individuals is a vulnerability as well as a tool.
- Schelling segregation depends on rigid identities and no randomness: when preferences are about changeable behaviour and 5% to 10% of agents move at random, a model by Xu, Clark and Pak reports substantial to nearly full integration; adding an income gap restored segregation (C-10, full text of the PNAS article). Spontaneous moves of satisfied agents also change the Schelling dynamics in other variants (C-10).
- Optimality at a phase transition can come from structure rather than from responsiveness, and the critical state may be evolutionarily unstable (C-12).
- Variability can be constructive: random choice of the interaction partner gives more cohesive groups than interacting with the nearest neighbour only, in a model (C-11), and noise in small groups lifts accuracy under correlation (C-09). Both are simulation or model results; neither is tested with a real or LLM decision maker (Inference).

**Plan-distinction check.** Local correctness versus global utility: C-01, C-02 and C-10 define the collective objective separately from individual correctness (agreement, segregation index). Accuracy versus probability quality: none of the cards uses calibrated scores; noise is injected by construction. Random versus structured error: C-03 (independent) versus C-09 (correlated) versus C-01/C-02 (adversarial). Factual versus preference: C-06 and C-10 involve conflicting preferences; C-08 and C-09 assume an objective quality. Variability versus benefit: C-09 and C-11 compare with a no-noise condition; C-04 does not claim benefit from noise.

### C-01 Byzantine Generals: agreement with arbitrary faulty members

1. **Source.** Lamport, Shostak, Pease (1982), "The Byzantine Generals Problem", ACM TOPLAS 4(3):382-401. https://lamport.azurewebsites.net/pubs/byz.pdf (author-hosted PDF). Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: full text (abstract, sections 1-2, theorems 1-4 located by text search; the text extraction has OCR-like letter spacing, proofs were not checked line by line). Used: abstract, "Impossibility results", Theorem 1, Theorem 2.
2. **System, decision, objective.** n generals (nodes), each sends and relays an order or value; up to m may be traitors. Collective objective: (IC1) all loyal lieutenants obey the same order; (IC2) if the commander is loyal, every loyal lieutenant obeys that order. Local correctness of a loyal node is defined by following the algorithm; the global objective is agreement.
3. **Error.** Traitors may do anything (arbitrary, including different messages to different recipients). This is a worst-case fault model, not noise. Injected by definition in the proof; not natural or measured.
4. **Dependence.** Traitors may collude and are not independent of each other; faults are unrestricted across agents and time. Assumes a fully connected synchronous network with reliable direct links (oral-message model). Shared information: all nodes know the algorithm.
5. **Mechanism.** Correction by redundancy and relaying with majority at each level (Algorithm OM(m)). Class: correct at system level (errors are bounded, not diluted).
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** Theorem: with oral messages, no solution works for three generals with one traitor; no solution with fewer than 3m+1 generals handles m traitors; OM(m) works for 3m+1 or more generals (Theorem 1). With unforgeable signed messages any number of traitors can be tolerated (Theorem 2). Effect size and uncertainty: not applicable (exact result). The paper also observes that when the decision is a majority vote, a small number of traitors can change the outcome only when loyal generals are almost evenly split, "in which case neither decision could be called bad" (source claim, section 1).
7. **Conditions and failures.** The bound is tight: with m traitors and fewer than 3m+1 nodes no algorithm exists. Cost grows with m (messages and rounds). Requires reliable links, known m and synchrony. The authors state that "bad plan" is hard to formalise and they do not attempt it (condition B).
8. **Evidence type.** Theorem (impossibility plus constructive algorithm). Widely replicated and extended in the distributed-computing literature; I did not verify replications myself. No code or data.
9. **Jev relevance.** Mechanistically analogous (as a worst-case baseline), not directly demonstrated. The fault model (adversarial, arbitrary) does not match an 80-90% accurate decider whose errors are not adversarial but may be correlated. Missing assumptions for transfer: a defined value domain, a way to relay and compare decisions, a bound m on faulty agents, a definition of "bad plan". Inference: the ratio 2/3 loyal is far below the 80-90% scenario but concerns unrestricted behaviour; a weaker fault model could need less.
10. **Cost.** Message and round overhead growing with m (exact counts not extracted; the paper's Theorems 3 and 4 concern partially connected networks and were not read in detail).

### C-02 W-MSR resilient consensus on robust networks

1. **Source.** Primary: LeBlanc, Zhang, Koutsoukos, Sundaram (2013), "Resilient asymptotic consensus in robust networks", IEEE JSAC 31(4):766-781. **The primary paper was not opened** (no accessible PDF found; see search log). Secondary, opened: Usevitch and Panagou, "Resilient Leader-Follower Consensus to Arbitrary Reference Values", arXiv:1802.09654, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.09654 (section I and section II.B restate the primary results with citations). Also Senejohnny, Sundaram, De Persis, Tesi, arXiv:1802.08457 (introduction only). Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: full text of the secondary source for the relevant statements; primary theorems taken as restated by them.
2. **System, decision, objective.** Networked agents each holding a scalar state; each normal agent discards the F largest values above and F smallest below its own (W-MSR, weighted mean-subsequence-reduced) and averages the rest. Objective: normal agents converge to a common value inside the convex hull of their initial values.
3. **Error.** Misbehaving agents: malicious (send the same value to all out-neighbours) or Byzantine (different values to different neighbours), per the secondary source. Adversary model: F-total (at most F misbehaving in the network) or F-local (at most F among any agent's neighbours). Injected by definition.
4. **Dependence.** Adversary may know the whole network and other nodes' states (stated in the search-result summary of the primary abstract, not in the opened secondary source). No independence assumed; bound on the count of faulty neighbours instead. Time-invariant or time-varying graphs treated in extensions.
5. **Mechanism.** Absorption by trimming (extreme values removed without identifying the sender), plus redundancy in graph connectivity ("network robustness"). Class: absorb/correct at system level.
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** Per the secondary source: under an F-total malicious model, (F+1,F+1)-robustness is necessary and sufficient for resilient asymptotic consensus under W-MSR; under F-local malicious or F-total Byzantine models, (2F+1)-robustness is sufficient. Network size and effect sizes: not applicable (theorem). Uncertainty: none.
7. **Conditions and failures.** The consensus value lies in the hull of initial values but is not controlled; the secondary source shows that merely adding F+1 reference "leader" agents in an (F+1,F+1)- or (2F+1)-robust network does not guarantee convergence to the leaders' value (counterexample in section II.B). Convergence to an arbitrary desired value is therefore not guaranteed by robustness alone. Secondary source (introduction) notes that in the asynchronous-clock setting only approximate consensus is obtained under their conditions.
8. **Evidence type.** Theorem; secondary-source restatement read, primary not read. Many follow-up papers (secondary sources list several variants: ARC-P, SW-MSR, DP-MSR). No code or data examined.
9. **Jev relevance.** Mechanistically analogous. Directly demonstrated only for scalar-state numeric consensus. Missing for transfer: numeric state to trim (decisions here are not ordered scalars unless encoded as such), a bound F on faulty neighbours (not an accuracy rate), and redundant graph structure. Inference: trimming extremes is a candidate "absorb" mechanism if Jev decisions can be mapped to an ordered quantity; for categorical actions there is no obvious analogue.
10. **Cost.** Requires graph redundancy (each node needs many in-neighbours; robustness grows with F), discards information from 2F neighbours each step, slower convergence than plain averaging (stated qualitatively only; not quantified in the sources I read).

### C-03 Noisy and unreliable-link average consensus: the bias-variance trade-off

1. **Source.** Kar and Moura (2008/2009), "Distributed Consensus Algorithms in Sensor Networks With Imperfect Communication: Link Failures and Channel Noise", arXiv:0711.3915v2, https://arxiv.org/pdf/0711.3915. Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: abstract, introduction and assumptions, theorem statements located (Theorems 3 and 4); proofs and numerical studies not read.
2. **System, decision, objective.** N sensors repeatedly average with neighbours over random links with additive channel noise. Objective: all states converge to the average of initial values.
3. **Error.** Link failures (packet loss, random topology) and additive communication noise. Injected as a model assumption (motivated by wireless sensor networks). Noise: independent zero-mean in the main assumptions; the paper also treats spatially correlated link failures and certain Markovian noise (appendix, stated in the introduction).
4. **Dependence.** Noise independent across time and across links in the main case (Assumption 2.1); Markovian noise in an extension; link-failure matrices independent over time with constant mean (the paper says it does not assume a distribution beyond that).
5. **Mechanism.** Absorption by averaging, with a designed trade-off: persistent (decaying) weights (A-ND) versus constant weights with restart and Monte Carlo averaging (A-NC). Class: absorb.
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** Source claim (abstract): consensus under noise has a bias-variance dilemma, running longer reduces bias but raises variance; A-ND achieves almost-sure convergence to the desired average (asymptotic unbiasedness) and the mean-square error of the limit is computed explicitly, at the cost of slow convergence. Shorter runs with more restarts give higher bias and smaller variance. Numeric effect sizes: unreported in what I read.
7. **Conditions and failures.** Requires connectivity in expectation (the paper's condition lambda2 of the mean Laplacian greater than 0). Fast convergence and low variance conflict. Persistent noise means plain constant-weight consensus does not converge to the true average; the final estimate is a random variable around it (source claim, abstract).
8. **Evidence type.** Theorem plus numerical study (not read). Peer-reviewed in IEEE Trans. Information Theory (venue stated from memory, not verified; the arXiv record shows only cs.IT). No code examined.
9. **Jev relevance.** Mechanistically analogous. Shows the quantitative structure of "errors average out" under independent zero-mean noise, including the dependence on independence assumptions. Not directly demonstrated for decisions: states are continuous, noise is additive. Missing: categorical decisions, error correlation across agents, a persistent shared bias (the averaging limit would then be wrong in expectation, Inference).
10. **Cost.** Convergence time and number of messages; the algorithm design trades speed against variance.

### C-04 Vicsek model and the noise-driven transition, with the later correction

1. **Source.** (a) Vicsek, Czirok, Ben-Jacob, Cohen, Shochet (1995), "Novel type of phase transition in a system of self-driven particles", Phys. Rev. Lett. 75:1226; arXiv:cond-mat/0611743, https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0611743. (b) Gregoire and Chate (2004), "Onset of collective and cohesive motion", Phys. Rev. Lett. 92:025702; arXiv:cond-mat/0401208, https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0401208. (c) Chen and Zhang (2007), "Comment on 'Novel type of phase transition...'", arXiv:cond-mat/0703532, https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0703532. Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: full text of (a) through its results; (b) abstract, model definition and parts of results; (c) first page.
2. **System, decision, objective.** N point particles at constant speed; each updates its heading to the mean heading of neighbours within radius r plus a random angle. Collective objective: polarisation (order parameter, mean velocity magnitude |v_a|). There is no individual correct action; the pattern is the object evaluated.
3. **Error.** Uniform random angular perturbation in [-eta/2, eta/2] (a) or white noise (b). Injected by construction; noise is the control parameter (temperature-like). (b) also compares angular and vectorial noise.
4. **Dependence.** Noise independent per particle and per step; interaction through local neighbourhoods; positions diffuse so neighbourhoods change.
5. **Mechanism.** Constructive variability is the wrong label here. The model shows that alignment absorbs noise below a threshold; above it order is lost. Class: absorption, with a sharp limit.
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** (a) Simulations with up to 10000 particles (a statement in the text: 4000 and 10000 particles close to the transition have about 5% statistical error from 5 runs). Critical noise eta_c(infinity) = 2.9 +/- 0.05 at density 0.4 (the "infinite-temperature" value is 2 pi); order parameter exponent beta = 0.45 +/- 0.07 reported as a continuous transition. (b) Finds the onset is discontinuous (first-order-like) in the original model and in cohesive variants; the apparent continuity comes from strong finite-size effects; hysteresis found; simulated N of 4096 and 16384 in the figure captions. (c) Notes a failure case: when neighbour contributions cancel the average direction is undefined ("direction annihilation"), with simulated probabilities plotted on an axis up to 20% for small cells, falling with density (exact values not extracted); this is a single comment, not replicated here.
7. **Conditions and failures.** Order needs high density or low noise; at high noise particles move randomly with some correlation. Near onset the system forms high-density travelling bands (from (b), abstract and body; the PRE 2008 follow-up, not opened, extends this). The transition is not a smooth dial: small noise changes near the threshold can switch the state.
8. **Evidence type.** Controlled simulation; the original continuity claim was corrected by a later simulation study (discontinuous); replications of the model are extensive (secondary evidence in the hits). Code and data: unreported in the papers read.
9. **Jev relevance.** Mechanistically analogous. The noise-versus-alignment phase transition is the standard example that a noise level can flip a collective from disordered to ordered. Inference: it does not show noise helping; the useful state needs low noise. Missing for transfer: Jev agents are not heading-copying particles; a mapping from LLM choice variability to a noise amplitude is not established.
10. **Cost.** None for the mechanism; costs of ordered motion (loss of exploration) are not assessed.

### C-05 Informed minority leading a group (Couzin et al. 2005)

1. **Source.** Couzin, Krause, Franks, Levin (2005), "Effective leadership and decision-making in animal groups on the move", Nature 433:513-516. https://www.nature.com/articles/nature03236. Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: **abstract only** (obtained through Europe PMC REST API; the Nature and Bristol pages were not readable). No figures or tables seen.
2. **System, decision, objective.** Group moves with simple local rules; a few individuals have information (a target direction). Objective: group follows the informed direction accurately; consensus when informed individuals hold conflicting directions.
3. **Error.** Not an error model. The uninformed individuals lack information; informed ones may not know who else is informed. The abstract does not describe noise.
4. **Dependence.** Information transfer without signalling through local interaction; informed individuals do not know whether they are in a majority or whether others exist.
5. **Mechanism.** Steering by a minority; consensus by averaging of conflicting directions.
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** Source claim: the larger the group, the smaller the proportion of informed individuals needed; a very small proportion is enough for great accuracy; groups reach consensus even when informed individuals do not know whether they are a majority. Numeric values: unreported in the abstract (not seen).
7. **Conditions and failures.** Not available from the abstract; failure modes (e.g. conflicting equal-size informed subgroups) not seen.
8. **Evidence type.** Model (simulation). Replication and code unknown from what I read.
9. **Jev relevance.** Mechanistically analogous (steering with few biased agents). Missing: Jev agents lack a spatial-alignment interface; "informed" would need an operational meaning (an agent with a prior or score).
10. **Cost.** Not stated.

### C-06 Uninformed individuals and democratic consensus (Couzin et al. 2011)

1. **Source.** Couzin, Ioannou, Demirel, Gross, Torney, Hartnett, Conradt, Levin, Leonard (2011), "Uninformed individuals promote democratic consensus in animal groups", Science 334(6062):1578-1580. https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.1210280 (abstract via Bristol repository https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/uninformed-individuals-promote-democratic-consensus-in-animal-gro and Europe PMC record). Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: **abstract only**; the Science page returned HTTP 403.
2. **System, decision, objective.** Groups (golden shiners in experiments, plus theory) with conflicting preferences; objective: consensus that follows the numerical majority.
3. **Error.** Not noise; conflicting interests and absence of information. Conflicts are among informed individuals with different preferences.
4. **Dependence.** Social interaction and attraction; details not seen.
5. **Mechanism.** Steering and its inhibition: uninformed individuals dilute a strongly opinionated minority.
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** Abstract claim: for a wide range of conditions a strongly opinionated minority can dictate group choice, but the presence of uninformed individuals returns control to the majority. The Science summary text from the web search (not independently read) says fish trained to a blue target in the majority and to a natural yellow preference in a minority; treat it as unverified. Numbers: unreported.
7. **Conditions and failures.** "For a wide range of conditions" means not for all; the exceptions were not seen. The abstract also notes that previous claims argued weakly informed groups are vulnerable to manipulation.
8. **Evidence type.** Theory plus controlled animal experiment. Replication unknown.
9. **Jev relevance.** Mechanistically analogous, and the lesson is a risk: a strongly biased minority can capture a group; neutral agents counteract. Missing: preference conflict, which the plan separates from factual truth-estimation.
10. **Cost.** Not stated.

### C-07 Emergent gradient sensing by a fish school (Berdahl et al. 2013)

1. **Source.** Berdahl, Torney, Ioannou, Faria, Couzin (2013), "Emergent sensing of complex environments by mobile animal groups", Science 339(6119):574-576. https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1225883 (abstract via Bristol repository https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/emergent-sensing-of-complex-environments-by-mobile-animal-groups/). Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: **abstract only**; the Science page returned HTTP 403.
2. **System, decision, objective.** Golden shiners moving in a light-gradient environment, individuals changing speed in response to local scalar light measurements and social interaction. Objective: the group tracks the preferred dark region.
3. **Error.** Individuals have noisy, local, scalar measurements; the abstract frames the result as emergent sensing rather than the pooling of individual estimates. Noise level not stated in the abstract.
4. **Dependence.** Social interaction couples the individuals.
5. **Mechanism.** Absorption and system-level sensing: gradient detection exists at the group level and not at the individual level (abstract claim).
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** Abstract claim: emergent problem solving is the predominant mechanism, "robust" group-level sensing arises. Group sizes, effect sizes and uncertainty: unreported (not seen).
7. **Conditions and failures.** Not seen.
8. **Evidence type.** Empirical experiment plus model; replication unknown.
9. **Jev relevance.** Mechanistically analogous to the claim that individually uninformative decisions can add up to useful global behaviour when interaction is present. Speculative for Jev. Missing: spatial coupling and shared environment.
10. **Cost.** The abstract says the mechanism needs only rudimentary cognition.

### C-08 Honeybee nest-site choice: independence plus interdependence

1. **Source.** List, Elsholtz, Seeley (finalised 2008; published 2009), "Independence and interdependence in collective decision making: an agent-based model of nest-site choice by honey bee swarms", Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B 364:755-762. Author preprint, https://personal.lse.ac.uk/list/pdf-files/honeybees.pdf (full text read; the preprint, not the published version). Related, abstract only: Seeley et al. (2012), "Stop signals provide cross inhibition in collective decision-making by honeybee swarms", Science 335:108-111, via Europe PMC. Accessed 2026-10-06. Used: Introduction, Simulations, Table 1, Discussion.
2. **System, decision, objective.** 200 scout bees; 5 candidate sites of fixed quality 3, 5, 7, 9, 10; each bee assesses quality with noise (log-scale reliability sigma) and either explores independently or follows others' advertisement (interdependence lambda). Objective: the swarm picks the best site (strong criterion: more than twice the support of the runner-up and at least 20% of scouts dancing).
3. **Error.** Individual quality assessment noise sigma (0.2 high reliability, 1 low). The model assumes an objective quality, so there is a ground truth. Noise is injected as a model parameter; its basis is "empirically motivated" per the authors.
4. **Dependence.** Individual assessments independent; communication creates dependence by design (recruitment proportional to advertisement). Time dependence: 300 time periods per run.
5. **Mechanism.** Absorption plus constructive interaction: independent noisy assessment feeds a positive-feedback recruitment that concentrates support on the best site. Class: absorb, and error amplification when independence is weak.
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** 250 simulations per cell. Table 1: with high interdependence (lambda 0.8) and sigma 0.2, best site chosen in 246/250 (98.4%) under the strong criterion and 250/250 under the weak one; with sigma 1, 199/250 (79.6%) strong and 237/250 (94.8%) weak (46 runs reached no consensus under the strong criterion). With medium interdependence (0.5): 41.6% (sigma 0.2) and 37.6% (sigma 1) strong, because most runs reach no winner under the strong criterion (58.4% and 62%). With low interdependence (0.2): 4.4% and 2.8% strong. Uncertainty intervals: unreported (counts only).
7. **Conditions and failures.** Source claim: without interdependence there is no snowballing of attention; without independence, "a consensus would still emerge, but it would no longer robustly be on the best nest site", and informational cascades follow (restaurant example). The model assumes an objective quality and ignores preference differences (the authors state this). Results are model predictions consistent with, not a fit to, the empirical observations of Seeley and Buhrman (2001), which I did not open.
8. **Evidence type.** Controlled simulation (Mathematica) with parameters motivated by experiments. Replication: unknown. Code and data: not stated in the preprint. Seeley 2012: an analytic model and experiments (abstract only) that cross inhibition resolves deadlock over equal sites.
9. **Jev relevance.** Mechanistically analogous, and the best structured example of a trade-off between independent noisy judgments and communication. Missing: an objective quality score and a recruitment channel among agents; independence between the judgments must hold, which is false if all agents are the same model with the same prompt (Inference).
10. **Cost.** Time to consensus (hundreds of periods in the model); no cost model beyond that.

### C-09 Noise in small groups under correlated information (Kao and Couzin 2014)

1. **Source.** Kao and Couzin (2014), "Decision accuracy in complex environments is often maximized by small group sizes", Proc. R. Soc. B 281:20133305. PMC4043084, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4043084/ ; abstract via Europe PMC. Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: full text of the introduction and the first results section (two-cue model, figure 1 caption); remaining sections not read.
2. **System, decision, objective.** Groups choose between two options by simple majority; each individual votes from one of several environmental cues. Objective: group accuracy (probability that the majority picks the superior option).
3. **Error.** Cue reliability r between 0.5 and 1; cues differ in observational correlation across individuals (low or high). Errors are natural properties of the model environment, injected analytically.
4. **Dependence.** Central variable. A high-correlation cue is shared by all individuals; a low-correlation cue is sampled independently. The paper cites earlier theory that correlation makes collective decisions worse.
5. **Mechanism.** Constructive variability (sampling noise in small groups avoids shared-cue errors) versus absorption by averaging (standard wisdom of crowds). Class: variability, within a majority-vote model.
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** Analytic and numerical comparison of group accuracy against group size. Abstract claim: only in a minority of environments does accuracy increase monotonically with group size; otherwise a finite, often small, group size maximises accuracy, and "it is the noise inherent in these small groups that enhances their accuracy". Example in the figure caption: N = 20, r_L = 0.55, p = 0.75 (an illustrative case, not the headline effect). Effect sizes across the parameter space: unreported in what I read. Uncertainty: not applicable (exact binomial calculation).
7. **Conditions and failures.** The effect needs a mix of correlated and independent cues; where the cues are all independent the standard result holds (the authors say so). Idealised: binary choice, majority rule, fixed voting probabilities.
8. **Evidence type.** Theoretical model (analytic with numerical evaluation). No experiment. Code and replication: unreported.
9. **Jev relevance.** Mechanistically analogous and directly on point for the plan distinction on dependence: shared error sources undermine large groups, and a no-noise comparison exists inside the model. Missing for transfer: whether LLM agents' errors resemble a shared high-correlation cue and whether the votes can be treated as binary and independent given the cue.
10. **Cost.** Smaller groups lose absolute accuracy in the benign (independent) environments; cost of extra agents otherwise unaddressed.

### C-10 Schelling segregation: mild preferences, strong outcomes, and what changes it

1. **Source.** Schelling (1971; 1978) original work not opened. Opened: (a) Gauvin, Vannimenus, Nadal (2009), "Phase diagram of a Schelling segregation model", Eur. Phys. J. B 70:293-304; arXiv:0903.4694, https://arxiv.org/pdf/0903.4694 (full text, introduction and model sections read). (b) Xu, Clark, Pak (2024), "Homophily, selection, and choice in segregation models", PNAS; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10873625/ (abstract, significance and the results paragraph on random movers read in the retrieved page text). (c) Hatna and Benenson (2012), "The Schelling Model of Ethnic Residential Dynamics", JASSS 15(1):6, https://www.jasss.org/15/1/6.html (**fetch summary only**; quotes and thresholds there are not independently checked). Accessed 2026-10-06.
2. **System, decision, objective.** Agents on a lattice relocate to vacancies when the share of unlike neighbours exceeds a tolerance T. Collective outcome: spatial segregation (cluster-based coefficient). Individual objective: satisfaction with the neighbourhood; the individuals do not prefer segregation.
3. **Error.** No decision error in the base model. Noise enters as moves by satisfied agents (a), idiosyncratic random relocation (b, 5% to 10% of agents), or spontaneous relocation (c, m = 0.01 per the summary). Injected by construction.
4. **Dependence.** Each move changes neighbours' satisfaction, so decisions are state-dependent and sequentially coupled; there is a feedback loop.
5. **Mechanism.** Emergent amplification of a weak local preference (steering by a modest bias), with noise as a modifier of the outcome. Class: steering and amplification.
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** (a) Source claim: strong segregation "can arise from rather weak individual preferences"; in their variant the system is dynamically frozen and mixed at low tolerance, segregated at intermediate tolerance, and mixed again at high tolerance; transitions are sharp and first-order-like up to vacancy density 50%; L = 50 lattice, quantities averaged over 30000 steps after equilibrium. They note that moves by satisfied agents add noise resembling thermal noise and avoid a known artefact of the original model, finite-size clusters. Moves can lower global utility. (b) Source claim: with behaviour-based homophily and random movers, five percent random relocation gives substantial integration and ten percent nearly full integration; with minority income at 40% of the majority and housing costs, segregation stays significant at period 1000 (abstract-level and results text). (c) Fetch summary: threshold for segregation near tolerance of about one third (F_sgr, "essentially lower than 1/2") and sharp change from mixed to segregated; liquid-like dynamics produce mixed states in which part of one group stays integrated. Uncertainty: unreported.
7. **Conditions and failures.** Segregation depends on identity-based preferences that are fixed; (b) shows integration when preferences are about changeable behaviour. (b) acknowledges a focus on behaviours that are easy to change. (c) summary: segregated and mixed areas do not coexist permanently in their framework. Low-tolerance systems freeze mixed instead of segregating (a), so "mild preference gives segregation" has a non-monotone phase diagram.
8. **Evidence type.** Controlled simulation; the original result has been replicated and extended widely (a: "proved robust with respect to various more realistic refinements", the authors' statement). Empirical validity of the model for real cities is contested (Clark and Fossett 2008 appeared in the search results but was not opened).
9. **Jev relevance.** Mechanistically analogous to the plan's steering question: a small statistical bias in each local choice can flip the aggregate, and the phase diagram has sharp thresholds (tipping points). Not directly demonstrated for LLM decisions. Missing: a mapping from model score to move probability, and calibration.
10. **Cost.** The cost of segregation is the collective outcome itself; the preference of individuals is mild.

### C-11 Randomness in neighbour choice promotes cohesion (Jadhav, Guttal, Raj 2021)

1. **Source.** Jadhav, Guttal, Raj (2021), "Randomness in the choice of neighbours promotes cohesion in mobile animal groups", arXiv:2112.02974v1, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.02974 (preprint; full text read through the model and results opening). Code stated at https://github.com/tee-lab/cohesion-in-collective- (URL truncated in the extracted text; not checked). Accessed 2026-10-06.
2. **System, decision, objective.** Spatial agent-based model; each agent asynchronously aligns with or moves towards one or k randomly chosen neighbours among its K nearest. Collective objective: group cohesion (average cluster size C by DBSCAN).
3. **Error.** The randomness is the random choice of the interaction partner (plus spontaneous turning). It is a modelled behavioural rule inspired by empirical fish and bat results, not a measured error.
4. **Dependence.** Choices independent per agent; interaction topology evolves with positions (graph analysis).
5. **Mechanism.** Constructive variability: random partner choice gives a well-connected, dynamic interaction network that keeps the group together.
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** Comparison conditions: nearest-neighbour-only (K = 1), random pairwise choice (k = 1, larger K), and Vicsek-like local averaging (k = K). Source claim: with larger groups, nearest-neighbour interaction no longer suffices and groups split into clusters of size 2 or 3, while random choice among K nearest gives cohesion near 1 "even with one individual"; random choice is better than interacting with only the nearest neighbour. Group sizes N = 3, 5, 30 appear in the text; replicates and uncertainty bands: unreported in what I read.
7. **Conditions and failures.** Depends on a topological neighbourhood (K nearest), a visual field with a blind zone, and asynchronous updates; variants without asynchronicity were tested (the authors say the results hold). No real animal data are tested here.
8. **Evidence type.** Controlled simulation with graph-theoretic analysis; preprint, no independent replication known; code available.
9. **Jev relevance.** Mechanistically analogous; supports the idea that variability in whom an agent attends to can be better than a deterministic rule, with the no-randomness comparison present. Speculative for Jev because the benefit depends on a spatial group-cohesion objective.
10. **Cost.** Not studied; the random rule uses less information per agent.

### C-12 Collective predator evasion and the criticality hypothesis (Klamser and Romanczuk 2021)

1. **Source.** Klamser and Romanczuk (2021), "Collective predator evasion: Putting the criticality hypothesis to the test", PLoS Comput. Biol. 17(3):e1008832, https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1008832 (printable PDF read: abstract and introduction; code stated at https://github.com/PaPeK/PredatorPrey, not checked). Ioannou, Guttal, Couzin (2012), Science 337(6099):1212 onward (abstract summary from search only, not opened) is the related empirical study. Accessed 2026-10-06. Read level: abstract, introduction, figure captions via text extraction; fetch summary supplied the 400-agent figure, which I did not re-verify in the PDF.
2. **System, decision, objective.** Schooling prey agents with an alignment strength, a predator pursuing the weighted mean direction of frontal neighbours. Objective: low predator capture rate (survival), plus polarisation and information transfer.
3. **Error.** No decision error; the variable is the alignment strength with noise, the order-disorder transition located near the critical alignment. Injected by construction.
4. **Dependence.** Spatial interaction (Voronoi neighbours per the summary), shared predator. Evolution at the individual level adds selection feedback.
5. **Mechanism.** Absorption by group structure (not constructive variability).
6. **Comparison, size, metric.** Abstract claim: schools at criticality perform best in predator-capture terms; this is "not due to optimal response" but due to spatial structure; individual-level evolution makes the critical state evolutionarily unstable because of spatial self-sorting. Capture rate is strongly anti-correlated with inter-individual distance (R = -0.69, figure 2 caption, as extracted). Population 400 agents and 76 attack simulations per fitness estimate (fetch summary, not verified in the PDF). Uncertainty: unreported.
7. **Conditions and failures.** Applies to fission-fusion groups of unrelated individuals with individual selection; qualifies the idea that sitting at a phase transition is a general optimum. The authors say previous theory used non-spatial models.
8. **Evidence type.** Controlled simulation (agent-based), code stated; replication unknown.
9. **Jev relevance.** Speculative. The point that the benefit of a "critical" operating regime may come from a side effect of structure, and that it may not be stable, is a caution against transferring the phase-transition story to a design that tunes noise. Missing assumptions: prey-predator geometry.
10. **Cost.** Not studied beyond capture rate.

## Cross-cutting observations (Inference)

1. The only cards that compare with a no-variability condition and report a benefit from variability are C-09 (small groups, model) and C-11 (random partner choice, simulation). Both are model results. The rest show tolerance of variability, not benefit.
2. The dependence assumption is the lever in nearly every card: independence of noise (C-03), independence of assessments (C-08), shared cues (C-09), bounded count of faults rather than independence (C-01, C-02). A repeated model across many agents corresponds to the shared-cue case unless sampling noise is injected, which is the plan's A2 assumption and remains unresolved.
3. Steering by a minority or a small bias (C-05, C-06, C-10) is shown in models and an abstract-only experiment; each card also names a way the same mechanism can be captured by an opinionated minority or tip into an unwanted state.
4. Control-theory cards give guarantees only under their fault model; none addresses semantic decisions.
