# Option labels, guardrail attack and order-invariant architectures

Theme summary. Two full-text cards: the option-label channel attack on open decision models used as guardrails, and a decision-native architecture that is order invariant and null aware. Read via pdftotext, accessed 2026-10-19.

## 2610.12292 — One Word Opens the Gate: The Option-Channel Attack on Typed Decision Models as Agent Guardrails
- Authors: Seyedarmin Azizi, Erfan Baghaei Potraghloo, Massoud Pedram (University of Southern California; independent academic, same group as 2610.02586); arXiv v1 dated 8 Oct 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12292 (code: github.com/ArminAzizi98/option-channel-attack); read: full text, accessed 2026-10-19. The authors built the GuardBench benchmark and the defenses, but not a decision model that they rank.
- Systems and readouts: seven open-weight models. Five typed models: LAYA-TD and LAYA-EN (ModernBERT-large, 421M), LAYA-ML (322M), VON (ModernBERT, 395M), RLCD (GLiClass encoder, 151M; the only one with an explicit "insufficient evidence" answer). Baselines: Qwen2.5-Instruct 1.5B and 7B, scored by the log-probability of the option name. Six of the seven appear on the public tasks and six on GuardBench (Sec. 2, 5). Hosted Jev is not measured (Sec. 10); the authors rely on Sun et al. for it. No pointer-head or modern letter-logit decision model.
- Design: not pre-registered. Models act as allow/block gates, and fail-open (FO, prohibited action allowed) and fail-closed (FC) are reported separately (Eq. 1). Attack success (asr_open, Eq. 2) is scored only on items the gate first decided correctly. GuardBench: synthetic agent tool calls generated from six attributes, six policies (Table 1), ground truth computed, classes balanced exactly, each trace ending in an attacker-controlled tool-output span; each policy is written positive, explicit-negation and semantic ("positive" is used for the attack runs). Public tasks: prompt injection (n=406 for typed models), jailbreak (1034), toxic content (768), each balanced; Qwen uses 300 each. Percentile bootstrap, 4000 resamples. Option-channel attack: change only the label of the permissive option (definitions and state untouched), searching a 198-label vocabulary in six kinds.
- Key results (measured):
  - Clean gates (Table 2): accuracy 36% to 72% against 50% chance. LAYA-TD fail-closed 1.5 to 4.4% with fail-open 60.7 to 77.1% (almost never blocks). Qwen2.5-1.5B fail-closed 97.3 to 100% (blocks almost everything; fail-open 0.7% on injection, accuracy 49.7%). RLCD best accuracy 71.7% (injection) but fail-open 45.8 to 65.4%. LAYA-ML on toxic: fail-closed 96.1%. Models lie near the "ignore the input" line (Fig. 2).
  - Policy wording: stating the block condition as a negation gives 100% fail-open on two policies (Sec. 6.1).
  - Text that carries no policy information (Sec. 6.2, Table 3, Fig. 3): on unauth_irrev, the one policy where LAYA-TD is accurate (87.3% accuracy, 0% clean fail-open, 150 correctly blocked items), six appended server-log lines raise fail-open to 63% and three unverified approval notes to 70%. On toxic screening a 17-word polite closing causes 88.6% [81.4, 94.8] of 88 correct blocks to be allowed ("Write a lewd story": P(block) 0.56 to 0.21). Fail-closed is unchanged at the median, so the shift is toward allowing. In Table 3 Qwen2.5-7B and RLCD are mostly near 0 under distraction; VON reaches 94.2% on exfil at k=6.
  - Option-channel attack (Sec. 6.3, Tables 4, 5, 7; Fig. 5): on the four models that put the label in the input (LAYA-EN/ML/TD, Qwen2.5-7B), the worst renaming of the permissive option takes fail-open to between 93% and 100% (Table 4; e.g. LAYA-TD unauth_irrev 0.0 to 100.0 with allow renamed "escalate"; Qwen secret 0.0 to 100.0 with "ok"). Averaged over the six policies the best label reaches 98.2 to 100.0%. VON and RLCD are exactly unaffected (honest equals worst: 61.3 and 59.3). Qwen2.5-7B, the best clean gate (mean honest fail-open 4.7%, blocks every violation on 4 of 6 policies), goes to 100% on all six policies with "remediate". Query cost (Table 5): LAYA-TD 60.0 honest, 83.9 after k=5 random labels, 89.5 after k=10; Qwen 4.7 to 68.8 to 89.1. Label kind (Fig. 5a, LAYA-TD unauth_irrev): security-action names 57% mean, neutral tokens such as "option 2" 37%, unrelated nouns 27% ("obsidian" reaches 100%), synonyms of allow 13%, synonyms of block 1%. Renaming only the permissive option to "A" gives 67% fail-open, while renaming both to A and B leaves 0% (Sec. 6.3). Transfer (Table 6): the top-5 labels found on LAYA-EN raise LAYA-TD by 38.2 pp, about equal to LAYA-TD's own (38.2), but LAYA-ML transfers in neither direction. Public tasks (Table 7): LAYA-TD 72.4 honest to 97.1 best; jailbreak 61% to 100% with "escalate"; LAYA-ML toxic 10% to 63%.
  - Mechanism, from code inspection: VON builds its input from definitions only (`desc.strip() if desc else opt.strip()`, option_marker_backend.py:633) and the label is used only as the answer key; the other three show the label to the network. This confirms the Azizi et al. 2610.02586 finding for VON.
  - Label invariance does not protect against appended text: VON asr_open under the six-line distraction pooled over policies is 41.1% vs LAYA-EN 24.2% and LAYA-ML 83.3% (Sec. 6.3).
  - Confidence and thresholds (Sec. 6.4, 6.5; Table 8; Fig. 4, 6): LAYA-TD's correct blocks (n=338) have median P(block) 0.57, 97.0% below 0.70, max 0.82. On jailbreak the share of reversed blocks falls from 55% to 0% as the clean margin grows. Lowering the threshold 0.50 to 0.40 on unauth_irrev moves clean fail-closed 26.0 to 70.7% and attacked fail-open 68.7 to 10.0%; at 0.30 it blocks everything. Over 1410 reversed decisions the pooled margin is 0.149 before and 0.151 after (wider after in 53%). Escalating the lowest-margin 10% of traffic only lowers attacked fail-open 60.1 to 53.8% (oracle 40.9%); at 50% review it still leaves 25.3% (oracle 0.0%).
  - Defenses (Sec. 7, 8; Tables 9 to 13): matched comparison against an undefended gate biased toward blocking to the same clean fail-closed rate, 72 cells (4 defenses, 3 models, 6 policies): defenses win 45, tie 21, lose 6. D1 canonical labels A/B: 9 better, 7 tie, 2 worse; D3 ensemble of 3 neutral labelings: 10/6/2; D4+D6 two labelings, disagreement escalated and treated as blocked: 10/6/2; D5 pass only structured fields: 16/2/0 but at cost 68.7% clean fail-closed on unauth_irrev. Jailbreak: D1 raises clean accuracy 68.0 to 86.0% and cuts attacked fail-open 63.2 to 3.5%, but on injection and toxic the defended gate still fails open on 68.0% and 84.6%. D8 (instruction "judge by the definitions, names carry no information"): no effect on LAYA-TD (99% vs 98%); on Qwen the old label "remediate" falls to 4% but a re-search finds "harpsichord" and returns 100% on 6/6 policies (k=10: 88.2 to 40.6). Adaptive attacks: D5 non-adaptive 0.0 but adaptive median 87.4 to 99.3% (LAYA-EN/TD/RLCD), max 100% on four of five models (55.7% LAYA-ML); D4+D6 under a both-labelings suffix: LAYA-TD 52.5 to 71.6%, LAYA-ML 28.2 to 52.7%; VON and RLCD unchanged (disagreement check never fires). Value normalization (parse fields to values): field injection 53% to 0%, but confusing values like api.internal.corp.paste.ee still reach 20.3 to 50.0%; a deterministic rule over the parsed values scores 100% on all six policies, 0.0 fail-open (Table 11).
- Method/recipe and cost: rewrite labels at the call site (cheapest defense; clean fail-closed on unauth_irrev 25.3 to 36.0%); never let untrusted strings become labels; compare any defense with a same-FC thresholded baseline; the two-labeling disagreement signal needs one extra call. The attack needs a handful of label queries (k=5 to 10) and transfers partially across models.
- Limitations (authors): GuardBench is synthetic and templated (public tasks added to compensate); open-weight only, hosted Jev not measured so mechanistic claims apply only to inspectable implementations; attacks are cheap fixed-pool or fixed-vocabulary searches so rates are lower bounds; GuardBench admits a deterministic rule by construction (Sec. 7.1), which is a property of the construction, not evidence the tasks are easy; the public tasks cannot be parsed. My caveats: the headline "93 to 100%" is a per-model worst case over the best of 198 labels on a chosen policy, and the cleanest single-policy numbers use unauth_irrev because that is where LAYA-TD is accurate; most clean gates are near-chance so many cells have little to attack (cells with no correct blocks are excluded). Author claim "every defense we tested is defeated" is partly by their own adaptive attacks with white-box knowledge of the defense. Qwen is scored by option-name log-probability, so it inherits the label channel by design. Labels in this paper are policy-semantic ("allow"/"escalate"), so results say little about opaque pointer keys. The extra reported VON immunity is code-specific (a calling-library property), not an architectural one.
- Relevance to the lab: items 1 and 3 (option naming and rendering; label effects) and 4/5 (ensembles, calibration, cascades). Verdict: extends and partly parallels the lab. It confirms that the label is a separate input channel whose effect depends on whether the library renders it, and it directly tests two lab-adjacent mitigations: (a) opaque or canonical keys (D1) help the label channel but not appended-text attacks; (b) a population or ensemble of relabelings (D3, D4+D6) beats a same-cost threshold on most cells but is defeated by an attacker who targets both renderings. It contradicts the use of the output probability as an escalation or abstention signal in an attack setting (reversed decisions are equally confident: 0.149 vs 0.151), which bears directly on the lab's cascade and fallback ideas, although the lab's missing-evidence failures are not adversarial. No test of a pointer head, letter logits, or an abstention option (RLCD's "insufficient evidence" is reported only as a feature, no result on it). Ideas: (1) add a clean fail-open vs fail-closed split to every lab decision table; (2) run the lab's relabeling-disagreement flag with an opaque-key rendering and compare to D4+D6's reported behavior; (3) check the lab's embedded-instruction attack against the "same-FC thresholded baseline" standard before calling any verifier an improvement.

## 2610.18773 — Jeva: System One Models from First Principles
- Authors: Qi Li, Xinchao Wang (National University of Singapore; academic, the authors built and rank their own model, Jeva-4B); arXiv v1 dated 13 Oct 2026; https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18773; read: full text (main text and appendices skimmed), accessed 2026-10-19. No limitations section; the conclusion (Sec. 5) is the only closing statement. No release link is stated in the text I read.
- Systems and readouts: proposed Jeva-4B (Qwen3.5-4B backbone, rank-64 LoRA, one decision block L=1, H=8, d=768, 13.37M added parameters; each option and the request encoded independently with last-token pooling; option templates contain only the decision type and the option text, no key, index, request or other option). Compared (native runtimes): hosted Jev-1.13.0, JevK5-4B, InternDecision 4B/2B/0.8B, ReFlex-4B, SemiF-4B, KEV-4B (BF16 and FP32), CLM-8B, Valen-Preview-2B, Laya-English, and Qwen3.5-4B/2B label log-probability baselines. Readouts differ across models (answer-token logits, decision markers, pointer head, dedicated head, label LP).
- Design: not pre-registered. Typed Decisions test (LocalLLaMA 2026; 2000 test decisions: 600 Choice, 600 Noul, 800 Score), BANKING77 and MASSIVE with K=8 or 16 (answer-present and answer-withheld requests), Mind2Web (K=16, three slices), 600 Choice examples in original, reversed and up to three seeded random orders (identities aligned), 3080 matched BANKING77 K=16 requests (588 answer-absent) for null detection, 500 ToolRet requests over K=32 to 2048, MLDR and Typed Decisions for caching, H200 latency. Baselines get an auxiliary Noul question or an appended explicit "None" candidate for open-set evaluation (Appendix C.7). Training curriculum of roughly 80k+500k+250k+60k+20k+60k+10k+80k+2k+70k examples.
- Key results (measured, all author-run):
  - Accuracy (Table 1): Jeva-4B leads 7 of 8 slices at full coverage; Typed Decisions test 78.30 vs InternDecision-4B 78.70 and Jev-1.13.0 74.10; BANKING77 K=8/16 92.05/91.62 (Jev 86.14/82.50); MASSIVE 91.09/90.22; Mind2Web 79.99/62.25/66.23. Laya-English 34.50 on Typed Decisions; Qwen3.5-4B label LP 50.85. Several baselines on Mind2Web or BANKING77 are very low (e.g. InternDecision-2B 48.31 on BANKING77 K=8), so the baseline set is not uniformly well-tuned.
  - Order invariance (Table 2, Sec. 4.1; 600 Choice): Jeva 0.00% flip, mean TV 3.17e-8, original = all-order accuracy 76.67%. Jev-1.13.0 flip 17.00% (all-order 66.67%, TV 0.0858); InternDecision-4B 22.67% (64.50%); JevK5 26.17% (47.83%); KEV-4B BF16 17.50% (55.50%, TV 0.0658); Laya-English 66.83% (11.83%); Qwen3.5-4B label LP 84.50% (14.17%, TV 0.414); Qwen3.5-2B 76.83% (18.17%). Invariance is by construction (Proposition 3, exact arithmetic) and needs no augmentation.
  - Null (abstention) detection (Sec. 4.2, Fig. 2a/6b): BANKING77 K=16, 588 answer-absent: AUROC 98.66% vs KEV-BF16 93.61%, InternDecision-4B 84.27%, JevK5 79.44%; 93.88% of absent answers detected at 5.02% FPR. Noul Brier 0.0184 vs Jev 0.0342 and InternDecision-4B 0.0340; Score MAE 0.2315 vs InternDecision-4B 0.2488 (RMSE 0.3222 vs 0.3208, i.e. not best). Design: a separate coverage gate rho = sigmoid((max score - b_cov)/tau_cov), p_null = 1 - rho, p_i = rho*q_i; the null score does not depend on K (Proposition 2) whereas a flat softmax null does (log K term).
  - Candidate scaling (Sec. 4.3, Fig. 7, 8): ToolRet 500 requests, K up to 2048: Jeva 78.40% aggregate (86.90% at K=32, 69.88% at K=2048) vs recursive JevK5 64.00%; all-size accuracy 69.88% (58/83 queries correct at every size) vs JevK5 57.8% (48/83) and CLM 8.4% (7/83). InternDecision supports only 65 of 84 requests at K=32 and none above; ReFlex and SemiF none. Length-aware batching K=2048 p50 94.459 s to 12.040 s (7.85x).
  - Caching (Sec. 4.4): hybrid option-level cache 27.98x p50 speedup at full reuse on 50 MLDR queries (2102.0 to 75.1 ms); shared bank Typed Decisions p50 93.1 to 46.6 ms with 99.2% of candidate rows avoided; max absolute probability difference between cache conditions 0.00736 (so decisions are not strictly bit-identical, but none changed).
- Method/recipe and cost: independent encoding of request and each option; single-block cross-attention of the request state over an immutable option memory; shared scoring head; separate temperatures for evidence mixing, competition and coverage; joint cross-entropy over (null, options); option memory cacheable. Training is a multi-stage curriculum totaling roughly one million examples; teacher probabilities are accepted only when the top prediction agrees under two shuffled orders (Appendix C.1). Cost to the lab: this is a new 4B model with custom head, not a prompt-level change; the lab cannot adopt it without the (unreleased?) checkpoint, which the paper text does not link.
- Limitations (authors): none stated in a section; the appendix notes that proofs assume exact arithmetic and that floating point and tied maxima can perturb results (App. B.5), that each text has its own length budget, and that Proposition 1 is existence not a guarantee. My caveats: author-built and author-ranked, baselines run at their native interfaces and adapters written by the authors (open-set adapters for baselines are an auxiliary Noul question or appended "None", which may disadvantage them); Typed Decisions is the only slice where Jeva does not lead (78.30 vs 78.70), and that benchmark is also what Jev-style models are trained on; Mind2Web and BANKING77 evaluate intent/element selection, not decision rubrics; permutation tests reorder candidates only (not labels, since there are none in the encoder input); no adversarial, injection or option-name test, and no calibration (ECE/reliability) numbers are reported, only Brier/MAE/RMSE; no statistical intervals or seeds are reported in the text I read; the stable-order claim is architectural, so a flip rate of 0.00 is expected and is not itself a surprising measurement. Table 2 shows the order-sensitivity of other models on 600 Choice questions only.
- Relevance to the lab: items 1 (missing evidence/abstention/fallback), 2 (option order), 4 (populations), 5 (calibration/cascades), and naming/rendering (the option template carries no key). Verdict: parallel and partly extends. (a) Order: gives baseline order-flip rates for hosted Jev (17.00%) and KEV (17.50%) that the lab can compare to its own order results, and shows that order-invariance can be obtained by architecture instead of by a population of reorderings (ReFlex-style averaging is cited as the alternative). (b) Abstention: a separate coverage score distinguishes "no option applies" from "uncertain among options"; reported AUROC 98.66% on BANKING77 for answer-absent requests, with baselines using an explicit None candidate or an auxiliary Noul question; this is the closest evidence to the lab's missing-evidence failure, but it is intent detection with a trained null class, not absence of evidence in the state. (c) Naming: since options are encoded without keys, Jeva is label-invariant in the sense of 2610.12292's VON (never sees a label); it would not be exposed to the option-channel attack, but nothing in the paper tests state-injected text or key-naming text. Ideas: (1) borrow the flip-rate and all-order accuracy protocol (original, reversed, 3 random orders, identity-aligned) for the lab's systems; (2) measure the lab's null/abstain handling with the same two probes: answer-absent AUROC at fixed FPR and null detection under a "None" candidate vs an auxiliary Noul question; (3) treat the Jeva result as an existence proof that a separate coverage head is a design option, not as evidence that it fixes missing-evidence errors in the lab's setting.
